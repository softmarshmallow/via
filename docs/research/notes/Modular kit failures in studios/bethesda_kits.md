# Bethesda Game Studios modular kits: what went wrong and what was changed

Scope: Fallout 3, Skyrim, Fallout 4, Fallout 76, Starfield, as told by the people who did the work. The headline rules already recorded elsewhere (512/256 footprints, pick a pivot, naming scheme, proof/graybox/build-out, loopback/stack/gap tests) are not repeated as findings; they appear only where a story needs them.

## 1. What specific kit failures do Burgess and Purkeypile describe? (what broke, when found, cost, change)

### Takeaway
The failures they tell are almost all contract failures between pieces, found when a second person used the kit in a way the first pair did not test, and each was answered by fixing the core piece or the kit's rule instead of adding a piece. The costliest stories are a Fallout 3 kit nobody could use because its joining rule lived in an unreadable name, a Fallout 4 kit that was built whole when only its pipes were wanted, and the move to wall/floor/ceiling granularity that multiplied object count by six.

### Cited Findings

**Fallout 3 "Utility / Bay" sub-kit: the joining rule was encoded where nobody could read it.**
- A Fallout 3 piece name carried a segment ("PRTT01") meant to say what the piece tiles with above, below and on each side. Level designers could not internalise it, so the kit was underused in the shipped game and part of the effort spent building it was wasted; the authors themselves annotate the segment as "No clue" [first-hand, read in full] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- This sub-kit tiled vertically (the "Mid" token marks a vertically tiling piece), i.e. the failure was in a kit with a more complex adjacency rule than the usual one- or two-axis kits [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Change: names must be readable by someone who has never seen the kit; conventions reused across kits and projects; a slightly obscure token ("1Way", "2Way") is allowed only when it sorts functionally similar pieces together and is used identically in every kit [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Later change (Fallout 4): compatibility information moved out of names and into editor-only "helper markers" drawn on the piece: outlines of walls and doorways, ceiling "handles", which way a door opens, which side of an asymmetric hall is which, which direction a directional piece must flow. Burgess describes these uses in a 2013 comment under the transcript [first-hand] — [2013 transcript, comments](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- In 2013 the helper markers shown were a mock-up; the feature did not yet exist in the editor. It shipped for Fallout 4 as points that pieces snap to by their pivot [third-party notes; the 2016 slide says only "Helper Markers: Alluded in 2013"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4); [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)

**The hallway corner with a stub: a loop that misses by half a footprint.**
- In a room-plus-hallway loop test the last door missed by about half a footprint. The first reaction was to ask for a half-length hallway. They call that a "patch-up" piece: it fixes one layout, bloats the kit, and adds a non-obvious usage rule. The real fault was the corner ("2-way") piece, longer on one side than the other; the fix was to rework that piece to sit inside the footprint [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Burgess repeated in 2018 that patch pieces subvert the kit's core logic and damage its long-term function [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)

**Sub-kit footprints that are not multiples (the 384 case).**
- Stated as a rule with a worked number, not as a named shipped kit: sub-kits may have different footprints but they must be multiples; a 384 room snaps to a 256/512 hall at first and only fails when the layout loops back, with gaps or overlaps. The point of the story is that the failure is invisible at the first join [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- 2016 restates this as caution about non-uniform tiling: a non-square piece is acceptable as long as it returns to the base grid when its run ends [2016 slides: "Be cautious with Non-Uniform Tiling"; elaboration is third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

**Alcoves outside the footprint: two halls cannot run side by side.**
- A hallway built to the full footprint had wall alcoves poking outside it; two such halls could not be placed adjacent. Level designers could have routed around it, but that would have put arbitrary restrictions on layout, so the piece was redrawn with the alcoves inside the footprint. They credit the proof stage with catching this before visual art existed [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Walls on the footprint edge leave no thickness: adjacent pieces become co-planar [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- 2016 gloss: if a piece must exceed the footprint, treat it as occupying two cells and reason in multiples [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

**Stacking: zero-thickness floors.**
- A hall kit that used the whole vertical footprint gave a co-planar ceiling/floor when stacked: visible paper-thin slab from a stairwell or hole, z-fighting flicker, hard optimisation and trigger placement, confusing editor view. Change: decide floor thickness up front. They say the destroyed, highly architectural spaces of Fallout 3 are where this mattered most [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)

**Pillars made from hall pieces.**
- Placing hall corners and 3-way junctions side by side to fake a pillared room produces "pillars" the artist never designed, and they usually look bad. Their point: somebody always tries it, so the kit's level designer must raise it early and the pair must decide whether the kit supports it [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)

**All-axis tiling kits.**
- A kit that tiles on every axis gives great freedom but was too complex to build and to use. Change: when vertical stacking is wanted, build a separate "shaft" sub-kit with a fixed horizontal size (Skyrim cave and Nordic shafts) [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)

**Ratway "pivot and flange": gaps by design, holes by accident.**
- Hall pieces rotated to arbitrary angles leave a gap at every joint; the gap is covered by an archway following walls, floor and ceiling. Costs they list: the archway cannot snap to both halls, so designers misplace it and leave tiny holes visible only from one angle; piece counts between two rooms are no longer predictable, so late adjustment is harder and layout becomes linear; clean, specific intersections are impossible. They judge it a perfect fit for the Ratway because that place is meant to look ancient and decayed; elsewhere in the talk they note the Ratway kit is used only twice [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Their stated lesson from the holes is procedural: play the level often instead of staying in the editor [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Burgess in 2018 names the trade: "swinging" pieces cost the kit its ability to tile along two horizontal axes [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Fallout 4's Steam Tunnel kit continued this approach and was used far more often; its floor level is not fixed in the module, so designers vary tunnel height and fill [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

**Directional (asymmetric) kits and the "de-twist".**
- Begun in Fallout 3: pieces that only join in one orientation (asymmetric halls, A/B sides). When two runs meet flipped, they do not join; a dedicated de-twist piece is needed to swap sides. More work for art and design; judged worth it for organic kits, usually not for architectural ones [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Their rule after these experiments: one logic quirk per kit; do not carry a rule-bend into every later kit, because art cost and designer error both rise [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)

**Fallout 4 Utility kit: a whole kit built when only the pipes were wanted.**
- The slide verdict is that it "Wasn't really worth it" and that all the team cared about were the pipes [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Detail: the kit was asymmetric and one-directional (a piece does not mate with itself turned 180 degrees), with bent-pipe pieces to end a pipe run into the next module's wall. Designers barely used it as a room kit; they took the pipes and mixed them into the Building kit. It was presented as an anti-example and a waste of the time spent [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- What they kept: the pipes became a "glue" kit that joins separate kits "fictionally, functionally, cosmetically" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Change in process: before starting kit X, ask what unique problem it solves and how it complements other kits [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)

**Too many kits: fourteen planned, five folded into one.**
- The first Fallout 4 list had fourteen kits (Retail, Institutional, Church, High-Tech, Industrial, Public Space, Steam Tunnel, Cave, House, The Institute, Neo-Classical, Office, Vault, Quarry). The time chart before and after shows Retail, Institutional, Public Space, Neo-Classical and Office replaced by one "Building" kit [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Reason on the slide: every kit carries overhead of lead time, on-boarding, iteration and maintenance [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Reasoning as noted: ten middling kits cost more than five kits of twice the complexity, because each kit must be learned and iterated; the folded kits share human-scale proportions and construction (basements, beams, bearing walls) and differ only in surface [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- The Building kit ended up in nearly every level, more than planned; its ancestor was Fallout 3's Office kit (credited to Robert Wisnewski), which level designers had used as a stand-in for locations whose own kits were not ready [third-party notes; slide 78 shows "Fallout 3 Office Kit (2008)"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4); [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)

**Granularity: fixing "pre-determined combos" cost 123 objects where 20 had done.**
- Old system: a corner piece was exported whole from 3ds Max (floor + two walls + ceiling as one mesh, e.g. BldWoodPSmHall2Way01); the slide calls this "lots of redundant work". New system: the same corner is an editor "pack-in" made of FlrOnly, Wall, WallCorner and CeilOnly pieces [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- Why: breaking the wall/floor/ceiling grouping gives destruction flexibility, interchangeability between kits, flexible layouts, and less dependence on combinations chosen in advance [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- The combinatorial problem as noted: a corner with a window then a door; the designer wants door then window, then a double door; each such request used to be a new exported piece, heading for hundreds or thousands of pieces [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Cost: the same simple room went from 20 placed objects to 123; the slide reads "More Flexibility / More Time-Consuming" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- Fix ("Preserving Workflow"): pack-in prefabs named with the old convention so layout is as fast as before, and any pack-in can be exploded into its granular pieces when a designer needs to swap one [2016 slides for the two states "Pack-In Prefab / Exploded Granular"; naming detail is third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Q&A: the engine combines pack-in meshes into one for performance, so the team did not worry about piece count [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

**Graybox pieces that were too generic.**
- A graybox that omits the features that must tile across footprints (a pipe down the middle of a room, deliberate asymmetry in a cave) proves nothing; those features have to be roughed in at graybox [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)

**Kit not ready: the level designer is a "non-starter".**
- Their own caveat on speed: with a good kit a Bethesda designer is the fastest in the industry; without one she cannot start at all. They state total dependence on kit art as a major downside of the approach [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Bethesda's editor has no primitive-geometry blockout; models come first, then levels [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Early in a project design used to sit idle waiting for art, art rushed its decisions, then changed them, and level work was thrown out or heavily revised. Change: get kits to a functional-but-ugly state first [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Fallout 4 formalised this as a gate: layout pass may start when "Core Kit Is Usable", and kit work focuses on "Trustworthy" function [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- A graybox Museum of Freedom existed in June 2012, about half a year after Skyrim shipped [2016 slide "First Pass (June 2012)"; identification of the level is third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

**Earlier studio: everything faced north, south, east or west.**
- Purkeypile on Bloodrayne 2 (Terminal Reality, before Bethesda): a naive modular approach, obvious to players because everything was oriented to the four compass directions [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Purkeypile in 2026: the earlier kits (Fallout 3 and his pre-Bethesda work) were strict 90-degree pieces that snapped together; for Skyrim he and Burgess loosened them [tool summary] — [The Examined Game interview](https://www.theexaminedgame.com/confessions-of-a-bethesda-veteran-nate-purkeypile-fallout-3-4-76-skyrim-starfield/)

### Inferences
- In every story the defect was in one core piece or one rule, and the tempting fix was an extra piece. Their consistent answer is that an extra piece is a second rule the user must remember. A tool that assembles from kits should treat "needs a filler piece to close" as a defect report against the kit, not as a layout problem.
- The PRTT01 story and the helper markers are the same lesson twice: adjacency rules that are not visible on the piece (in the name, or in the artist's head) are not used. Fallout 4 moved them onto the piece as editor-only geometry.
- The 384 and corner-stub failures share a property worth recording: they pass a pairwise join test and fail only on a closed loop. Pairwise compatibility is not sufficient evidence that a kit is sound.
- Granularity is a trade, not a win: finer pieces removed the combinatorial explosion of exported variants but multiplied placement work sixfold, and had to be paid back with a prefab layer that can be exploded on demand.

### Gaps
- No source gives hours or weeks lost for any single failure. Costs are stated qualitatively ("wasted effort", "not worth it").
- The 2016 talk's spoken account could not be read first-hand (see the header). The Utility kit and granularity stories are confirmed by slide text; the surrounding detail is one note-taker's.
- No first-hand account was found for Fallout 76 or Starfield kits specifically (footprints, pivots, joins).

## 2. What do they mean by "kit-bashing", "kit jamming", "mashing"? When did it work and when did it fail?

### Takeaway
They use the word in two senses: combining two kits to make a third look (encouraged), and using a piece for a job it was not made for, an "arthack" (shipped in large numbers but forbidden early because it hides gaps in the kit). It worked where the result could be loose and organic or where a shared transition existed; it failed at clean architectural joins, and the response was to build small kits whose only job is to sit between other kits.

### Cited Findings
- Sense one: mixing and matching kits is "sometimes called kit-bashing, kit-jamming, or a kit mashup", and is to be actively encouraged as a defence against repetition [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Sense two: "kitbashing or arthacks" are using art in a way it was not intended, e.g. turning a wall on its side as a stand-in bridge, or abusing a piece of furniture to fake a missing piece [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- They ship with many such hacks, and done well they stretch limited art. But in the first (layout) pass they are discouraged, because a hack this early masks a shortcoming of the kit that should be left visible and fixed in the kit [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- Worked: Dwemer halls bashed with ice caves gave an instant change of tone; Dwemer interiors mixed with exterior elements [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Failure modes they name for unforeseen combinations: bad intersections and lighting problems; not every combination looks good or makes sense. The artist's job is to join the conversation about what "almost works" and could work with a small tweak [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Purkeypile, 2026: once kits were loosened, he had to review every dungeon so results did not look wrong; designers combined kits in unexpected ways (a castle inside a cave), some artists objected, and he thought the results were memorable [tool summary] — [The Examined Game interview](https://www.theexaminedgame.com/confessions-of-a-bethesda-veteran-nate-purkeypile-fallout-3-4-76-skyrim-starfield/)
- Shell-based caves: standard grid shell pieces plus freely placed walls, pillars and balconies. Intersections are sometimes awkward; the remedies were an art-review process over actual usage, and lighting and ambient occlusion to hide the joins. Their judgement is that players do not notice those small intersections [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Layered inserts, as retold in 2016: a square grid cave corner is hidden by a floor-to-ceiling insert; the insert has ugly seams and gaps of its own; those are hidden by rock columns; so the fix is several layers deep. The same trick was used in Fallout 4 for industrial buildings (GNN Plaza is the slide example) [third-party notes; slides confirm "Use Layered Inserts w/Base Kits" and "Fallout 4 – GNN Plaza"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4); [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- Where jamming fails: pivot-and-flange "relies on jamming things together", so it breaks down when clean, specific intersections are wanted, and its cover pieces leave pinholes [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Snap-to-reference (a rotated piece becomes the local grid for what follows) is the tool that made off-axis work possible; it creates gaps between groups at different angles, which must be bridged [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Response 1, standard doorways: one door-frame size for the whole game lets kits join without unique transition pieces and gives AI and animation one standard [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- In Fallout 4 the single and double door sizes were fixed at the start and drilled into the whole team, since near the end everyone helps dress levels; the note-taker presents this as something not done properly before [third-party notes; slide confirms "Use Standardized Transitions"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4); [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- Burgess 2018: a standard doorway/window/portal system is the "saving grace" in a game with many logically distinct kits [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Response 2, glue kits: starting with the project after Skyrim they built small kits that exist to make transitions between kits and sub-kits (platforms, tunnels, ledges), made "with kit-bashing in mind" [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Response 3, plug and socket (Fallout 4): predefined "socket" holes that accept several "plug" pieces, to maximise transition flexibility [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Before that, a new piece had to be made for each transition between two kit types; a plug does not care which kit is on either side. The speakers reportedly called it obvious in hindsight [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Burgess's 2018 rule for when kits should share logic: use the same logic for kits that replace each other or will be combined heavily (pristine and destroyed versions of the same rooms); give isolated environments their own logic. The catch he names: shared logic makes kits compatible and also makes repetition more visible [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Scale of mixing in Fallout 4: some locations use seven or eight kits at once [third-party notes, Q&A] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

### Inferences
- "Kit-bash" as placeholder and "kit-bash" as design are treated oppositely: the first is banned until the kit's contract is proven, the second is designed for. A tool should distinguish "pieces from two kits joined through a declared transition" from "a piece used against its declared role".
- The three responses form a progression from pairwise to shared: per-pair transition pieces (pre-Fallout 4), a single door standard, then a socket/plug interface that any kit can implement. Each step reduces the number of pieces that must know about two kits at once.
- The loose techniques moved quality control from the kit (checked once) to each use (checked every time, by review). Purkeypile's "review every dungeon" is the running cost of that.

### Gaps
- No first-hand account was found of kit-bashing causing collision or navigation-mesh defects specifically. The nearest statements are that co-planar floors make trigger placement and optimisation hard, that the narrowest passage is kept to two character widths to avoid pathing trouble (2013), and that navmesh is deferred until layout is stable (2014). The Level Design Book adds that collision is one of the biggest issues to stress-test (third-party summary, https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics/modular).
- Dimensions of the Fallout 4 socket holes, and which kits implemented them, are not given in any source I could read.

## 3. "Art fatigue": how did repetition show up to players, and which remedies worked or did not?

### Takeaway
Players notice repeated dressing before repeated structure, and they notice the grid itself (right angles, identical proportions) even when art varies. Prefabricated dressed rooms failed (Oblivion); what they say worked was granular pieces under hand layout, cross-kit mixing, same-footprint variants, material swaps, combinable damage halves and decals. Hero pieces were pushed to last.

### Cited Findings
- How it showed: with median Steam play time over 100 hours, players notice the same rock, farmhouse or tapestry dozens of times [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Oblivion: with half Skyrim's team, the dungeon artists made "warehouse" cells of fully lit, cluttered rooms to copy, paste and rearrange. Players called the dungeons "cookie-cutter". Their observation from screenshots: repeated clutter is noticed first, repeated architecture second, especially in first person [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Change from Fallout 3: warehouse cells abolished; a level design group staffed; tools built so spaces are built from the most granular art available [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Fallout 3 dev diary: every space was to be built by hand and made unique in some way, down to not duplicating the arrangement of trash on a table, as a direct answer to the Oblivion criticism [first-hand, read in part] — [Fallout 3 dev diary](http://blog.joelburgess.com/p/fallout-3-dev-diary-level-design_28.html)
- The grid shows: a rules-based grid kit "will always feel like a kit"; everything is at 90 degrees and the player senses the system. This is what pushed them, from Fallout 3 on, to bend rules [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Equilateral (cube) footprints are the most versatile and tend to look the most repetitive [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Player reaction under the 2013 post: one commenter says the grid makes every hallway, door, ledge and street the same proportion; another says the same village over and over made Skyrim boring quickly; others say dungeon variety was Skyrim's biggest gain over Oblivion [third-party: reader comments, not developers] — [2013 transcript, comments](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Remedy, decouple kit from content: do not bind a kit to one enemy type or mood; let the kit be the architecture and other elements give identity. Repeated in 2018 with the example of a posh castle kit dressed with grimy enemy props [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html); [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Remedy, a light unique touch per location: Skyrim's roughly ten large Dwemer dungeons would have been one-note; one got lightweight unique assets and enemies found nowhere else [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Remedy, variants with the same role and footprint, suffixed 01/02/03 so they sort together and swap easily [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Fallout 4 added Ctrl+mouse-wheel to replace the selected piece with its neighbour in the list, described on the slide as embracing the existing workflow and a huge time saver [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Remedy, material swaps: the Building kit's variants (Brick, Colonial, Wallpaper, Wood Panel, Bare Wood, Deco, Concrete, Unique) share geometry; a swap changes textures only, "not an architecture change", and new ones take very little time [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- All Building kit material versions were added in about two weeks; most time went to the damaged variants [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- The Level Design Book's summary of the same point: texture variants are cheap because the module geometry does not have to be tested and validated again [third-party summary] — [Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics/modular)
- Damage repeats worst: the slide states the wall-hole problem as visual quality versus repetition. Solution: many small pieces mixed and matched for a multiplicative effect [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Detail: the Building kit has 11 wall-with-hole variants, each only one side (half) of the hole, because the other side of the wall may belong to another kit; 11 x 11 gives 121 pairings [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Floor and ceiling damage could not be handled by variants of whole tiles; the solution was tile-friendly "free" platforms that can be raised and lowered independently, called a "useful option for demanding cases" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Decals and greebles (large "gross" decals, vents, stains) are listed as "useful to obscure kit patterns" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Hero pieces: tempting, impressive, and a distraction. A hero piece is used a few times; a standard piece hundreds or thousands of times. It blocks only the handful of places that need it, so it comes last [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- 2016 priority order on the slide: utilitarian core, then variants, then hero pieces; "Generic = Versatile"; and "LD: Use Placeholders to Prove Need" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Boston Public Library story: the designer's advance list of must-have unique pieces did not include the reading room's windows and ceiling; building the level from standard pieces first showed that everything on the list could be replaced by standard pieces and that those two things could not [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- A steel-mill quest scene is shown as rich and memorable with no unique set piece at all, only standard kits, lighting and composition [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Fallout 4 Wasteland Task Force anecdote: an early, incomplete test produced repetitive content (the same burning tires and bathtubs), which showed the team it lacked a class of assets (military), which were then added [tool summary of a transcribed documentary] — [Making of Fallout 4 statements](https://fallout.wiki/wiki/Fallout_4_Developer_Statements/The_Making_Of_Fallout_4)
- Journalist's reading, not a developer account: Oblivion's gates tired because palette and pieces never varied; Skyrim's Dwemer ruins and a Fallout 4 DLC vault that reuses vault pieces inside a theme-park ride felt fresh through kit mixing [third-party] — [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/fallout-4/bethesda-open-world-design)

### Inferences
- The Oblivion warehouse and the Fallout 4 pack-in are both prefabs. The difference they draw is that the warehouse room was copied as a dressed, lit, closed unit, while the pack-in is undressed structure that can be exploded and altered. Prefabrication failed at the level of dressed rooms and was reintroduced at the level of bare structural groups.
- The remedies that scale are multiplicative ones: split a visible feature into independently chosen halves or layers (hole sides, wall/floor/ceiling materials, decals) so the count of looks is a product, not a sum.
- Repetition has two layers that need different answers: repeated instances (answered by variants and swaps) and a visible lattice (answered only by changing the join rule: local grids, flanges, free-placed layers). Variants do nothing for the second.

### Gaps
- No measurement of what players noticed is given beyond anecdote and the 100-hour play time; there is no study, only the designers' observation.
- No developer account found of a remedy for exterior repetition (the "same farmhouse") beyond the general ones above.

## 4. Exterior and settlement kits: what was harder outdoors than in dungeons?

### Takeaway
The clear first-hand story is about Boston: buildings made as large pieces worked in Fallout 3 because each district was a walled-off cell that could be reshaped alone, and failed in Fallout 4's connected city where moving one building moved its neighbours; the answer was a much finer exterior building kit built in stacked layers, with interiors joined to the street without a load. Roofs, slopes and odd angles are not discussed in any source I could open.

### Cited Findings
- Fallout 3, Washington: early prototypes of a city laid out in the open world were a performance nightmare and also blocked the gameplay scenarios the team wanted, so the city became separate neighbourhoods with streets buried under rubble and joined through metro tunnels [first-hand, read in part] — [Fallout 3 dev diary](http://blog.joelburgess.com/p/fallout-3-dev-diary-level-design_28.html)
- The 2016 slides name this "Cloistered Neighborhoods / Separate from Capital Wasteland" and score it: "Fallout 3: Isolated – Easy to Cut & Reshape" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- These enclosed open-air cells were called "gardens" inside the studio, in contrast to dungeons; a fan-made map of them looks like a subway diagram, which the speakers used to show how badly they broke immersion [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Fallout 4, Boston: "Fallout 4: Interconnected – Everything Had to Fit – Required More Layout Flexibility", with the conclusion slide "Modular Workflow" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- The "Hard Living" slide lists what had to be overcome downtown: encounter pacing, POI/exploration density, combat and detection and enemy AI, performance/occlusion/sightlines, iteration and metrics; and ends "Everything took longer than expected" [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- The plan had been to treat each neighbourhood as a "super-dungeon" owned by one designer and one artist and expected to take a few times longer than an ordinary location. It did not work: designers clashed over work spilling past neighbourhood borders; a change in one district forced changes in the next and often the one after; time went into making streets and districts meet; and the first idea of using buildings as whole meshes failed [third-party notes; slides confirm the process steps "Neighborhoods / Ownership / Iteration"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- The same notes say this broke the schedule and moved the game's release by a year. **I found no second source for the one-year claim; treat it as unverified.** The slide says only that everything took longer than expected [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Earlier exterior kits were coarse. Whiterun's houses were modular, but as large modules usable only in Whiterun, often joinable in only one way, made by one artist for that one city. Fallout 3's downtown kits were made of very large pieces [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Change: Boston's buildings were made from small modules, far smaller than Fallout 3's. It was several times more work and needed more people, but it let artists bend building shapes to the irregular streets, and the notes say it reduced the pain of filling the city [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- The exterior "Deco" building kit is built in horizontal layers; the slide labels show Lobby Level, Lobby Trim and Roof Trim [2016 slides]. The notes give the full stack as foundation, lobby/ground floor, lobby trim (canopy), repeating floor layer, roof [third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Interior/exterior transitions: "Able to move freely between the two / No longer a load" [2016 slides]. The notes describe this as the first time the studio could make walk-through buildings on a city street [third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Boston's layout order: coastline and scale first, then skyline (anchor buildings, relative placement, orienting landmarks), then thoroughfares, then neighbourhoods, then ownership, then iteration; "Authenticity > Accuracy"; priorities were building heights, street widths, architectural styles and navigational flow [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- A graybox skyline of box buildings existed in March 2013, and its proportions were kept to ship [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Why Boston resists a grid: unlike a planned grid city, it grew along irregular paths, so blocks are not rectangles [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Scale: Todd Howard says world scale was often a bit wrong early and changing it mid-development was a problem, so the team now sketches the world on paper, then builds topography and walks it [tool summary of a transcribed documentary] — [Making of Fallout 4 statements](https://fallout.wiki/wiki/Fallout_4_Developer_Statements/The_Making_Of_Fallout_4)
- Diamond City (Purkeypile, 2026): many buildings are unique, with openings cut into other meshes so interiors are visible from outside; he pushed a circular layout so shops are reachable without loads; only one shop is a separate load [tool summary] — [The Examined Game interview](https://www.theexaminedgame.com/confessions-of-a-bethesda-veteran-nate-purkeypile-fallout-3-4-76-skyrim-starfield/)
- Starfield's Neon (Purkeypile): he laid it out wanting open shops with loads only for homes; the shipped city has more loads than he intended, and he guesses, without knowing, that a section was split off for performance [tool summary] — [The Examined Game interview](https://www.theexaminedgame.com/confessions-of-a-bethesda-veteran-nate-purkeypile-fallout-3-4-76-skyrim-starfield/)
- Starfield's planets (Purkeypile): he calls the result rearranged chunks rather than generation, saw repeated dungeons on the main quest, and would have built a handful of planets with bespoke layouts [tool summary; widely reported] — [The Examined Game interview](https://www.theexaminedgame.com/confessions-of-a-bethesda-veteran-nate-purkeypile-fallout-3-4-76-skyrim-starfield/); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/rpg/the-designer-of-diamond-city-little-lamplight-and-starfields-neon-thinks-bethesdas-galaxy-was-just-too-big-i-would-have-done-a-handful-of-planets-with-bespoke-layouts/)
- Small outdoor success: Skyrim's platform kits (pieces that sit on top of other surfaces) were also used on their own in the exterior as points of interest [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- The Fallout 4 Industrial kit started from concept art that was "not a kit": shapes too smooth and irregular, which had to be regularised to the chosen footprint, starting with gray concrete enclosure blocks that set the building's outline [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)

### Inferences
- The exterior lesson is about the cost of a late change, not about geometry: in a walled cell a coarse building piece is cheap to move; in a connected street grid every building is constrained by its neighbours, so pieces must be small enough to absorb a local change without pushing it outward. Coarse kits are tolerable only where their surroundings can be reshaped freely.
- The layered building kit (base, ground floor, trim, repeating floors, roof) is the exterior counterpart of the interior wall/floor/ceiling split: both separate parts that vary independently.
- The ownership-by-district model failed at the borders. Ownership of the seam between two authored regions was the unassigned thing.

### Gaps
- **No first-hand account found** of roofs, slopes, non-square angles or corners in exterior kits, nor of the inside-bigger-than-outside mismatch in Skyrim towns. A search returned only modder and fan commentary on the mismatch (for example https://forums.nexusmods.com/topic/6808362-mod-to-fix-bigger-on-inside-than-outside-buildings/); no developer explains it.
- No developer account found of Fallout 76's exterior kits or of Starfield's outpost and city kits at piece level. The only Starfield material is Purkeypile's layout and load comments.
- The footprint and piece sizes of the Fallout 4 Deco kit are not stated in any source I could read.
- A reader asked under the 2013 post whether kit systems had been tried for open-world areas; there is no reply there.

## 5. Player-facing kits: Fallout 4 workshop, Fallout 76 C.A.M.P., Starfield outposts and ship builder

### Takeaway
I found almost no developer account of snapping or connection-point problems in the player-facing builders. What exists: the Fallout 4 workshop was a cut-down subset of the developer kits and was nearly cut altogether, and in 2025 Bethesda removed most of Fallout 76's snap, support and collision rules, which is evidence by action that the rules had been the problem.

### Cited Findings
- Fallout 4's workshop kit was assembled from existing assets; players were given somewhat under a hundred pieces rather than the roughly 500 the developers had. Burgess said he expected the gap between developer kits and player kits to narrow over time [third-party notes, Q&A of the 2016 talk] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Jeff Gardiner (lead producer): the workshop features were first made with the mod community in mind, were "big, scary new tech", and he thought them the feature most likely to be cut [tool summary of a transcribed documentary] — [Making of Fallout 4 statements](https://fallout.wiki/wiki/Fallout_4_Developer_Statements/The_Making_Of_Fallout_4)
- Per-piece dynamic destruction was "Kit-Compatible" and, per the notes, worked best with the player's own building pieces [2016 slides for the first part; third-party notes for the second] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Fallout 76, official notes (2025): three placement modes were added. Snap mode behaves as before; collision mode does not auto-snap but still checks collisions; free mode has neither snapping nor collision [first-party release notes, tool summary] — [C.A.M.P. Revamp release notes, 2025-09-23](https://fallout.bethesda.net/en-US/news/fallout-76-c-a-m-p-revamp-release-notes)
- Same notes: pieces that used to require snapping may be left unsnapped; "over-snapping" (two pieces on one snap position) is allowed, for example to get wallpaper on both sides of a wall; walls can be built under a floor; upper floors can attach to walls without a staircase; things that required a foundation no longer do; objects may float, intersect or be unsupported; power cables may pass through objects. Still ground-bound: crops, extractors, items needing water [first-party, tool summary] — [C.A.M.P. Revamp release notes](https://fallout.bethesda.net/en-US/news/fallout-76-c-a-m-p-revamp-release-notes); [Inside the Vault, test server article](https://bethesda.net/article/5xquFqAxRnrd7s3TcNBQ9j/inside-the-vault-c-a-m-p-building-and-combat-public-test-server)
- Same notes: removing a piece no longer risks leaving part of a structure inaccessible [first-party, tool summary] — [C.A.M.P. Revamp release notes](https://fallout.bethesda.net/en-US/news/fallout-76-c-a-m-p-revamp-release-notes)
- The stated motive is freedom to build as players want; the notes do not explain why the old rules existed [first-party, tool summary] — [Inside the Vault, test server article](https://bethesda.net/article/5xquFqAxRnrd7s3TcNBQ9j/inside-the-vault-c-a-m-p-building-and-combat-public-test-server)
- A "Replace" mode was added so a placed piece can be swapped without scrapping and rebuilding; the article calls it work in progress [first-party, tool summary] — [Inside the Vault, test server article](https://bethesda.net/article/5xquFqAxRnrd7s3TcNBQ9j/inside-the-vault-c-a-m-p-building-and-combat-public-test-server)
- Player reports, not developer accounts: Fallout 4 forum threads complain that pieces snap everywhere except where wanted and that a third piece will not snap to two joined ones, and describe a "guide piece" workaround [third-party, search-result summary] — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/377160/discussions/0/496881136907750951/)
- Player reports, not developer accounts: in Starfield's ship builder the game, not the player, decides where doors and ladders appear between joined habitat modules; players reverse-engineered a preference order among connection points and a popular mod lets doors be placed by hand [third-party, search-result summary] — [Dot Esports guide](https://dotesports.com/starfield/news/starfield-ship-building-door-and-ladder-placement-explained)

### Inferences
- The Fallout 76 change list reads as an inventory of the old contract's rules: mandatory snap, one occupant per snap position, support from below, foundation required, stairs required for upper floors, no intersection, and connectivity checks on removal. Each rule that was dropped was presumably blocking legitimate builds more often than it prevented bad ones. This is my reading of the release notes, not a developer statement.
- The developers' own late-game behaviour (shipping with many arthacks, free-placed layers, intersections hidden by lighting) is the same freedom that players were first denied and later given.
- The Starfield door complaint is a case of an implicit join rule: two modules connect, and a third thing (the doorway) appears by a rule the user cannot see or steer. It mirrors the PRTT01 failure on the developer side.

### Gaps
- **No developer account found** of how Fallout 4 workshop snap points were designed, tested or went wrong. The commonly repeated story that the workshop began as a game-jam project by one programmer appears only in a listicle (https://gamerant.com/fallout-4-things-never-knew-about-building-settlements/) and I did not verify it.
- **No developer account found** for Starfield's outpost builder or ship builder at the level of snap points or module joins. Searches returned only player guides, a cut-feature interview about ship-building economy, and forum threads.
- The Noclip Fallout 76 documentary was not opened; a search did not indicate it covers snapping.

## 6. What happened when a kit's contract changed late (pivot, footprint, door position)?

### Takeaway
They describe it as the worst thing that can happen to a kit: every placed instance inherits the change at once, and for a pivot the repair is by hand, per instance. No migration tool is described anywhere; the defence is entirely procedural: freeze function before visuals, prove it on one finished piece, and gate layout on the kit being "trustworthy".

### Cited Findings
- Changing a pivot later means manual updates to hundreds of instances, and is worse if the pivot has already changed once before [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- A kit bug is not like an ordinary asset bug: an obvious fix to a bad pivot can cure the reported case and create new problems in hundreds of other placements nobody thought to check. This is why the kit artist must hold the whole rule system in her head for the whole project [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- The same mechanism is also the benefit: an export is a "fly-by deployment" to every level at once, so an artist can see new art in real use without building a test area [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Burgess 2018: pivot location, base footprint size and transition metrics "can be a disaster" to change, breaking hundreds or thousands of layouts as the change propagates [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- Burgess 2018: an artist may not see why changing the tiling edge of a piece is a big deal; the hurdle is teaching the difference between functional polish and visual polish, and getting artists to accept that proportions are committed early [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)
- 2016, as noted: once chosen, the pivot must never change, because a change ruins the level work done with it. The note-taker adds that this had happened at Bethesda more than once when a modeller updated a pivot not knowing the piece was already in levels; it is unclear whether that sentence is the speakers' or the note-taker's own [third-party notes] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Art-direction change after variants exist is the other late change they warn of: build one visually complete piece before any variants, because adjusting one piece is far cheaper than adjusting a kit; the changes to anticipate and avoid are those touching footprint, snap rules and pivot [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- The promise the process makes to level designers: at first pass the kits will keep evolving visually, but "the functional details - pivot points and such - will remain the same", so layout work will not be undermined [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- They admit the promise does not always hold: if graybox went well, final art swaps in seamlessly, but unforeseen problems can still undermine layout done during build-out [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Late additions are also a contract change: kit pieces that come online very late are hard to work back into existing spaces, which is why designers spend extra time early feeding back on the kit [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- They budget for it: later users always expose problems the first designer-artist pair missed, so kit polish time for course corrections is planned in advance [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Fallout 4 gates: "Core Kit Is Usable" before the layout pass and "Layout Is Reliable" before the gameplay pass; kit work is to focus on "Trustworthy" functionality, front-loaded in the art schedule [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4/59770460)
- Things fixed game-wide before any kit is started, so they never become a late change: door-frame size, narrowest traversable width (two characters), the slope AI can walk (60 degrees) and the slope that looks good (30 to 45), and any jump, fall or cover heights the game depends on [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Tools that ease a swap, though none repairs a moved pivot: same-footprint variants with numeric suffixes, Ctrl+wheel replacement of a selected piece, and pack-ins that can be exploded to replace one member [first-hand and 2016 slides] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html); [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- The Level Design Book, summarising these talks, suggests building a tool for swapping module types to speed art pass and remesh; this is the book's suggestion, not a Bethesda account [third-party summary] — [Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics/modular)

### Inferences
- The absence of any migration tooling in a studio that built tools for much smaller annoyances (mouse-wheel swap, layers) suggests they judged a contract change unrecoverable in practice and spent the effort on never needing one. For a tool that stores placements as data, this is the open opportunity: a placement recorded against a named joint or cell, not against a raw pivot transform, could survive a pivot or footprint revision that Bethesda's instances could not.
- "Especially if the pivot has changed before" implies the repair is not idempotent: instances fixed by hand after the first change carry offsets that a second change invalidates differently. This is my reading of one clause.
- They separate a kit's properties into two classes with different change rules: functional (footprint, pivot, snap rules, tiling edges, door metrics, names) frozen at proof/graybox; visual (mesh detail, texture, variants) open until ship. The failures occur when someone treats a functional property as visual.

### Gaps
- No concrete incident is told first-hand with a kit name, date and cost for a late pivot or footprint change; the statements are general.
- No account at all of a late change to door position or door-frame size.
- No tool or script for re-fitting existing layouts after a kit change is described in any source I could open.

## 7. How did level designers and kit artists divide ownership, and where did the hand-off fail?

### Takeaway
Their answer to hand-off failure is to have no hand-off: a kit artist and a level designer are assigned to each kit together from the first day, the designer's job is to break the kit and then to be its spokesperson to the rest of the team, and the artist's job is to hold the rule system and refuse pieces. The failures they report are the classic art pass that silently changes gameplay, kit artists who are rare and unwilling, requests that bloat the kit, and district ownership in Boston that left the borders to nobody.

### Cited Findings
- The failure they are reacting to, told as an industry-wide story they say they also lived: design blocks out a level, hands it to art, art polishes something it was not involved in, and it comes back with cover turned into poles too thin to hide behind, a sight-blocking wall turned into chain-link, and bridge supports across a planned firefight. They call hand-off a main fault line and admit to their own "communication problems and finger-pointing" [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Division on a kit: one kit artist and one level designer per kit, in daily contact, each an "ambassador" for a different set of needs [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- The designer's duty is to stress-test in non-ideal conditions, since testing a kit only as intended teaches nothing; the artist delivers pieces as soon as they are barely functional [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- The pair decides together what the kit does and does not support, because other designers will hit those limits later and should find an answer already exists [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- They note that stress-testing can feel combative [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- At build-out the kit's level designer presents the kit to the other designers (thinking, status, plans); only then do others start using it [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Requests: it is easy for an artist to say yes to every request, and the kit becomes bloated and unmanageable. For each request ask whether something existing already serves and whether the use case is worth a piece [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Fallout 4 put the burden of proof on the designer: use placeholders to prove a unique piece is needed before asking art for it [2016 slides] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Staffing: Skyrim's dungeons were made by eight level designers and two full-time kit artists. Kit work needs art skill, tool skill and an understanding of the editor and design workflow; they have worked with excellent artists who make good kits and refuse to, and call the right person a "unicorn" [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Artists who wait: some new environment artists will not release a piece until they know everything about how it will be used, which makes them a bottleneck; the answer is to release proportions and function first and finish looks later, seeing the pieces in real levels [third-party notes; the slide says "Unblocking Work: Minimize LD roadblocks / Get Real Use Cases ASAP"] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4); [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt)
- Artists' discomfort with unforeseen use: some are uneasy seeing their art used in ways they did not plan; the stated requirement of the job is to step back, allow it, and keep it in mind when making the art [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Review instead of hand-off: levels are reviewed by the artists whose work is used in them, alongside peer and lead reviews [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- Art pass late in a level: the designer is generally hands-off while artists do lighting, clutter and effects, but must not be too hands-off; artists are to be treated as collaborators with an ownership stake, not "janitorial staff" cleaning up after designers [first-hand] — [2014 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2014/07/gdc-2014-transcript-iterative-level.html)
- What the level designer owns day to day (Steve Massey, Bethesda level designer, on Fallout 76): he assembles the kit pieces himself and so controls much of how the space looks, then works with environment art on final visuals; he contrasts this with studios where designers hand a gray blockout to art [tool summary of a podcast transcript] — [Four Kitchens podcast](https://www.fourkitchens.com/blog/podcast/the-future-of-content-episode-14-video-game-level-design/)
- Outsiders see only the result: people using the released editor find kits impersonal and restrictive, because they get the finished kit without the collaboration that shaped it; "kits don't simply appear in the editor one day" [first-hand] — [2013 transcript](http://blog.joelburgess.com/2013/04/skyrims-modular-level-design-gdc-2013.html)
- Boston: each neighbourhood was owned from concept to finish by one designer and one artist; the model broke at the borders, with disputes over whose streets and buildings had to move [third-party notes; slides confirm "Ownership" as a process step] — [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Fallout 4 extended the kit idea to props: a "Machine Kit" of greebles, fins and tanks. The level designer blocks the machine's space functionally and an artist later handcrafts the swap [2016 slides]. The notes say about 400 machines were built this way, with concept artists doing assembly, against roughly two weeks for a unique asset [third-party notes] — [2016 slides](https://archive.org/stream/GDC2016Burgess/GDC2016-Burgess_djvu.txt); [dtf.ru notes](https://dtf.ru/gamedev/3788796-modulnyi-dizain-fallout-4)
- Burgess after leaving, 2018: some techniques that worked at Bethesda were hard to bring to his next studio, and he concludes there is no one-size-fits-all method [first-hand] — [80.lv 2018](https://80.lv/articles/building-huge-open-worlds-modularity-kits-art-fatigue/)

### Inferences
- Ownership is split by what kind of property is at stake, not by phase: the artist owns the rule system and may refuse pieces; the designer owns the list of supported and unsupported uses and must be able to state it. Both are documentation duties held in people's heads; the only written forms mentioned are names and, later, helper markers.
- The designer who helped build the kit acts as its first user-support contact. When modders received kits without that person, the kits read as arbitrary. A kit shipped to outside users (or to an automated assembler) needs its supported and unsupported uses written down, because the ambassador does not ship with it.
- Two different ownership failures appear: over a piece (art changes a property that design depended on) and over a seam (two owners, one border). The pairing model addresses the first; nothing in the sources addresses the second except making pieces smaller.

### Gaps
- No account names a specific kit where the artist-designer pairing broke down, or what was done about a particular disagreement.
- Nothing found on how kit ownership worked on Fallout 76 (a second studio joined) or Starfield (several studios), which is where a hand-off across teams would be most likely to fail.
- How the studio recorded a kit's supported and unsupported uses for later designers (wiki, document, word of mouth) is not stated.
