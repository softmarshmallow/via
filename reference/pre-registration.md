# Pre-registered reference sample

Status: **registered at the commit that introduces this file
(2026-08-21)** — the commit is the registration act — before any town
on this list —
other than the six pilot towns ADR 0010 Decision 2 pins to the fitted
partition — was fetched or measured (research 0012 §5.4; ADR 0010
Decisions 2 and 4). This document is the authority the fetch tooling
derives partitions from; a town not listed here is refused. Amending
the list after measurement has begun is recorded in place with a
dated note and never reassigns a measured town's partition.

The sample: **138 towns** across the five reference classes — 132
registered here plus the six pilots — each chosen by **documented
origin, never by appearance**, each with a fixed study centre chosen
so the 300 m protocol disc (v1.1) reads the class-defining fabric.
Selection and verification used web documentation and Nominatim
geocoding only: no Overpass extract was fetched, no town was
measured, and no street-network statistic was consulted. Candidates
were researched and then adversarially verified (origin claims
checked, centres reverse-geocoded and corrected, unsupportable rows
rejected); residual concerns are recorded under Limitations.

## Class definitions (by documented origin)

- **organic pre-modern core** — street fabric grown by accretion
  before ~1800 without an overall surveyed plan. Excludes charter
  foundations, post-fire replanning, and war-rebuilt cores.
- **planted pre-modern grid** — founded by charter or act with a
  surveyed plan before ~1800 (bastides, Edwardian new towns, terre
  nuove, Renaissance ideal towns, Laws-of-the-Indies foundations).
- **19th-century survey plat** — platted under formal survey in the
  19th century, documented by plat, railroad, colony or NRHP records.
- **mid-20th-century suburb** — mass-produced low-rise residential
  subdivision, roughly 1945–1975, documented developer or agency;
  excludes high-rise estates.
- **contemporary informal** — documented informal origin: self-built
  without formal planning or tenure at formation, largely post-1950.
  Settlements surveyed at formation (planned invasions with laid-out
  grids) are excluded by definition. Relocation-origin settlements
  qualify when residents were moved to unserviced land they then
  built themselves — the pilot Kibera profile.

## The held-out split (mechanical, by rule)

At least 30% of each class is reserved for validation and never used
for fitting (ADR 0008 D6; 0012 §5.4). To keep the split beyond
discretion, it is computed, not chosen: within each class, every
non-pilot town is keyed by the blake3 hash of its lowercase `Town`
slug (UTF-8 bytes), sorted by ascending hex digest, and the first
⌈0.3 × (class size including pilots)⌉ towns are **held-out**; all
others, and all pilots (ADR 0010 Decision 2), are **fitted**. The
resulting shares are 30.8–33.3% per class. The Partition column below
records the outcome; the rule above reproduces it.

## Coverage flags (0012 §9.1; protocol v1.1)

The Coverage column records the OSM building-footprint expectation at
registration time: `community-mapped` (a named mapping campaign is
documented), `expected-good`, or `unverified` — a vocabulary defined
here, satisfying protocol v1.1's requirement that unverified towns be
flagged. This is an
acquisition-time flag, not a measurement: the protocol's 30-footprint
floor and the coverage-verification step decide at measurement time
whether building characters are computed. Street characters are
unaffected.

### organic pre-modern core — origins and study centres

- **albaicin** (Albaicín, Granada): Founded as the Zirid Alcazaba Qadima when Zawi ibn Ziri moved the capital from Elvira to Granada in 1013, and accreted as the city's hillside quarter under Zirid and Nasrid rule, keeping its Islamic street net after 1492. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 314bis, Alhambra, Generalife and Albayzín, Granada (Albayzín extension 1994); Arabic sources on the 1013 Zirid foundation.* Centre: Plaza Larga, the quarter's own market square, deep in the upper Albaicín (reverse geocode confirms Plaza Larga, Albaicín Alto); contamination note: the Darro slope enters the southern disc edge.
- **albarracin** (Albarracín): Seat of the Banu Razin taifa of al-Sahla from the early 11th century and of the Azagra lordship after 1170, its ridge fabric accreting inside the Islamic-medieval walls. *Source: Conjunto Histórico declaration of Albarracín (Monumento Nacional, 1961 decree); Encyclopaedia of Islam entries on the Banu Razin taifa.* Centre: Plaza Mayor of the walled ridge town (reverse geocode confirms Plaza Mayor); contamination note: the Guadalaviar gorge puts unbuilt slopes in much of the disc (smallest fabric in this set).
- **bamberg** (Bamberg): Henry II raised Bamberg to a bishopric in 1007 (documented imperial diplomas) and the burgher Inselstadt accreted along the trade road between the river arms without an overall plan; the core was spared in WWII. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 624, Town of Bamberg (1993), ICOMOS evaluation; 1007 foundation diplomas of the bishopric.* Centre: Grüner Markt, the market spine of the Inselstadt burgher town between the Regnitz arms (reverse geocode confirms Grüner Markt, Inselstadt); medieval street net under baroque frontages (building-stock caveat is the generic 0012 §5.3 one).
- **berat** (Berat): Fortress town continuously occupied since ancient Antipatreia whose documented Ottoman-era quarters Mangalem (below the 13th-century Byzantine castle walls; King Mosque of 1492) and Gorica accreted below the citadel without a plan. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 569bis, Historic Centres of Berat and Gjirokastra (Berat extension 2008), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Mangalem quarter below the castle (reverse geocode confirms Kol Myzeqari, Mangalem; the point sits toward the quarter's lower edge, so the disc reads Mangalem and Gorica across the Osum); contamination note: the modern boulevard enters its eastern edge.
- **caceres** (Cáceres): Almohad walled town of the 12th century (walls on Roman foundations), retaken in 1229, whose intramural fabric accreted as an unplanned town of seigneurial houses through the late Middle Ages. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 384, Old Town of Cáceres (1986), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Plaza de Santa María, the principal church square of the intramural town (reverse geocode confirms Plaza de Santa María, Ciudad Monumental); the disc reaches the arcaded Plaza Mayor at the wall's edge and old extramural quarters beyond.
- **chefchaouen** (Chefchaouen): Founded 1471 by Ali ibn Rashid al-Alami as a mountain stronghold against the Portuguese and grown by accretion with Andalusi refugee settlement after the fall of Granada in 1492. *Source: Encyclopaedia of Islam (2nd ed.), art. Shafshawan; founding of 1471 by Ali ibn Rashid al-Alami.* Centre: Place Outa el-Hammam between the kasbah and the Grande Mosquée, the medina's principal square (reverse geocode confirms Place Outa Hamam, Souika); the 20th-century town to the west stays largely outside the disc.
- **dinan** (Dinan): Castle town attested by the 1065 siege embroidered on the Bayeux Tapestry, whose plateau town accreted within the 13th-15th-century walls above the Rance. *Source: Bayeux Tapestry, siege-of-Dinan scene (1065 campaign); Site patrimonial remarquable de Dinan (secteur sauvegardé), Ministère de la Culture.* Centre: Place des Merciers, the timber-framed market core of the intramural plateau town (reverse geocode confirms Place des Merciers, Intra-Muros); the walled upper town dominates the disc.
- **dinkelsbuhl** (Dinkelsbühl): Royal estate at a Wörnitz ford first documented 1188 as burgum Tinkelspuhel in a charter of Frederick Barbarossa; urban-history literature characterizes the core as grown, not planned (funnel-widened market streets, no rectangular plaza), with 14th-century wall-ring extensions; entirely war-spared (Romantic Road survivor). *Source: 1188 charter of Frederick I Barbarossa; Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Ensemble Altstadt Dinkelsbühl; Dehio, Handbuch der deutschen Kunstdenkmäler: Bayern.* Centre: Weinmarkt at the Münster St. Georg, centre of the walled Altstadt (reverse geocode confirms Weinmarkt); the wall circuit keeps the disc on pre-modern fabric, and the disc's core predates the 14th-century Stadterweiterungen.
- **evora** (Évora): Islamic Yabura (documented from 715 to the 1165 conquest by Geraldo Sem Pavor) whose radial-organic intramural fabric accreted inside successive wall rings without re-planning. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 361, Historic Centre of Évora (1986), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Praça do Giraldo, the market square of the organic core (reverse geocode confirms the Fonte Henriquina on Praça de Giraldo); disc stays well inside the 14th-century wall circuit.
- **fes-el-bali** (Fes el-Bali (Fez)): Founded by Idris I in 789 with Idris II's foundation on the opposite bank in 809 as chronicled in the Rawd al-Qirtas; the medina accreted around the Qarawiyyin mosque (859) into the archetypal unplanned Islamic urban fabric. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 170, Medina of Fez (1981); Ibn Abi Zar, Rawd al-Qirtas (14th-century chronicle of the Idrisid foundations).* Centre: Place Seffarine beside the Qarawiyyin mosque and library, deep inside Fes el-Bali's densest fabric, far from any modern breach (reverse geocode confirms Seffarine Square, Fès-Médina).
- **figeac** (Figeac): Formed around the abbey of Saint-Sauveur founded in 838 by Pepin I of Aquitaine (the rival 755 foundation charter is a documented forgery), the merchant town accreting around the monastic core from the 9th to 13th centuries. *Source: Site patrimonial remarquable (secteur sauvegardé) de Figeac, Ministère de la Culture; 838 foundation of Saint-Sauveur under Pepin I of Aquitaine.* Centre: Place Carnot, the medieval market (halle) square of the burgher town on the Célé's right bank (reverse geocode confirms Place Carnot); dense unplanned fabric fills the disc.
- **gjirokastra** (Gjirokastër): Accreted downhill from the 13th-century citadel (town attested as Argyrokastro in 1336) into quarters of fortified stone tower houses around the Ottoman bazaar crossroads. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 569, Historic Centres of Berat and Gjirokastra (2005), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Qafa e Pazarit, the Old Bazaar crossroads below the citadel, verified against the Bazaar (Memi Bey) Mosque at 40.0739, 20.1381 ~50 m away (the OSM neighbourhood label here, 'Hoxhagrad', is an admin-tag quirk, not a location error); contamination note: steep sparse slopes at the disc edge.
- **goslar** (Goslar): Merchant town accreted beside the 11th-century imperial palace precinct on the wealth of Rammelsberg mining documented from the 10th century; war-spared. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 623, Historic Town of Goslar and Mines of Rammelsberg (1992), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Marktplatz with Rathaus and Marktkirche, core of the medieval merchant town (reverse geocode confirms Rathaus, Markt, Altstadt); disc stays deep inside the war-spared Altstadt.
- **guimaraes** (Guimarães): Fused by accretion from two documented 10th-century nuclei — the monastery founded by Countess Mumadona Dias c. 950 (her testament of 26 January 959) and the castle of São Mamede she had built by 968 — into an unplanned medieval town. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 1031, Historic Centre of Guimarães (2001), ICOMOS evaluation; Mumadona Dias's 959 testament and 968 castle codicil.* Centre: Largo da Oliveira, the market/church square of the medieval lower town between the two documented founding nuclei (monastery below, castle hill above); reverse geocode confirms Largo da Oliveira at this point.
- **hall-in-tirol** (Hall in Tirol): Salt-boiling settlement documented from 1232 (salina near Thaur), named Hall by 1256 and granted town law in 1303 by Duke Otto of Carinthia-Tyrol, its upper town accreting on the Inn terrace around the salt trade. *Source: Österreichisches Städtebuch, Tirol volume (Hall in Tirol); documentary mentions of 1232 and 1256; town law of 1303.* Centre: Oberer Stadtplatz with parish church and Rathaus, core of the upper-town terrace above the Inn (reverse geocode confirms Oberer Stadtplatz); one of Austria's largest intact old towns.
- **matera** (Matera): The Sassi accreted as cave-and-masonry quarters on the Gravina rim from the early Middle Ages (archbishopric documented 1203), a continuously inhabited unplanned fabric until the 1952 evacuation law. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 670, The Sassi and the Park of the Rupestrian Churches of Matera (1993), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Piazza Duomo on the Civita spur between Sasso Barisano and Sasso Caveoso (reverse geocode confirms Piazza del Duomo, Sassi di Matera); contamination note: the planned 17th-19th-century Piano enters the north-west disc edge.
- **moulay-idriss** (Moulay Idriss Zerhoun): Pilgrimage town grown by accretion around the tomb and zawiya of Idris I (settled at Zerhoun in 789, d. 791) across the twin foothills of Khiber and Tasga. *Source: Ibn Abi Zar, Rawd al-Qirtas; Archnet site record, Moulay Idriss Zerhoun.* Centre: Sanctuary square at the mausoleum of Idris I (OSM tomb node at 34.0550, -5.5228; a pedestrian market way adjoins immediately west), at the saddle between the Khiber and Tasga hills; verification moved the centre here from a town-level geotag ~360 m west that fell on the sparse western slope.
- **nordlingen** (Nördlingen): First documented 898 as the Carolingian royal court Nordilinga (transferred that year to the bishops of Regensburg), it accreted radially around market and St. Georg inside two preserved wall rings; war-spared. *Source: Diploma of Emperor Arnulf, 898, confirming transfer of curtis Nordilinga to the bishops of Regensburg; Bayerisches Landesamt für Denkmalpflege, Ensemble Altstadt Nördlingen; Dehio Bayern.* Centre: Marktplatz beside St. Georg, hub of the radial-concentric organic plan; disc stays well inside the complete wall circuit (the Marktplatz way geocodes at exactly this point; Nominatim's suburb label 'Baldingen' here is an admin-hierarchy quirk, not a location error).
- **quedlinburg** (Quedlinburg): First recorded in a 922 diploma of Henry I; after Otto I founded the collegiate convent on the Burgberg in 936 the market settlement below grew by accretion into the Altstadt, war-spared with ~1,300 timber-framed houses. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 535, Collegiate Church, Castle and Old Town of Quedlinburg (1994), ICOMOS evaluation; 922 diploma of Henry I.* Centre: Marktplatz of the Altstadt, the organic market settlement below the Burgberg (reverse geocode confirms Marktplatz); contamination note: the planned 12th-century Neustadt enters the disc's eastern edge.
- **safranbolu** (Safranbolu): Ottoman caravan station on the east-west trade route documented from the 13th century, whose Çarşı quarter accreted around the market, hans (Cinci Hanı, 1645) and mosques on the valley floor. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 614, City of Safranbolu (1994), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: The Çarşı market square at Cinci Hanı, centre of the Ottoman lower town in the valley confluence (Cinci Hanı geocodes at exactly this point); Bağlar and the modern town sit on the plateau outside the disc.
- **san-gimignano** (San Gimignano): Via Francigena ridge town first documented in 929 that accreted between its two squares and tower houses and fossilized when the 1348 plague ended its growth. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 550, Historic Centre of San Gimignano (1990), ICOMOS evaluation (first documentary mention 929).* Centre: Piazza della Cisterna at the junction of the ridge routes (reverse geocode confirms Piazza della Cisterna, centro storico); the walls keep the disc on medieval fabric with olive slopes beyond.
- **sarlat** (Sarlat-la-Canéda): Town accreted around a Benedictine abbey attested under Pepin I of Aquitaine (817-838) and restored in 886, growing into an unplanned monastic-seigneurial town at its 12th-14th-century apogee. *Source: Secteur sauvegardé de Sarlat, PSMV 1964 (the first designated under the Malraux law), Ministère de la Culture; abbey attestations in the Périgord monastic record.* Centre: Place de la Liberté, the market square of the monastic town beside the cathedral (reverse geocode confirms Place de la Liberté); the PSMV perimeter comfortably contains the disc's core.
- **siena** (Siena): Accreted from the early Middle Ages along three ridge spines converging on the Campo at the Via Francigena crossing; the commune's 13th-century statutes regulated but never re-planned the fabric. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 717, Historic Centre of Siena (1995), ICOMOS evaluation; W. Braunfels, Mittelalterliche Stadtbaukunst in der Toskana (1953).* Centre: Piazza del Campo, the market place where the city's three ridge spines meet (reverse geocode confirms Il Campo); organic fabric dense in every direction well beyond the buffer.
- **stein-am-rhein** (Stein am Rhein): Market settlement grown gradually between monastery and Rhine bridgehead after Henry II relocated St. George's Abbey from Hohentwiel here in 1007 (imperial diploma; market rights 1094, town rights 1267); the 1945 stray bombing was repaired without re-planning. *Source: Historisches Lexikon der Schweiz, art. Stein am Rhein; ISOS Federal Inventory of Swiss Heritage Sites (national importance); 1007 relocation diploma of Henry II.* Centre: Rathausplatz, the market spine of the Städtli beside the abbey (reverse geocode confirms the Städtli); contamination note: the Rhine and bridgehead take the southern part of the disc.
- **tetouan** (Tétouan): Refounded 1484-92 by Andalusi exiles under Ali al-Mandari after the 1399 razing, then grown by accretion within its late-15th-century walls through the 18th century with no surveyed plan (a refoundation, not a surveyed re-planning; UNESCO describes main streets linking the gates with accretive fabric between, largely untouched by later intervention). *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 837, Medina of Tétouan (formerly known as Titawin) (1997), ICOMOS evaluation.* Centre: Point inside the walled medina, El Hammar area (reverse geocode confirms Médina de Tétouan (ancienne Titawin)); contamination note: Place Hassan II and the Spanish Ensanche fringe enter the western disc edge.
- **toledo** (Toledo): The intramural fabric is the organic accretion of the Islamic madina (711-1085) with its cul-de-sac quarter structure, retained essentially without re-planning after the Castilian conquest of 1085. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 379, Historic City of Toledo (1986); L. Torres Balbás, Ciudades hispanomusulmanas.* Centre: Plaza del Ayuntamiento, the cathedral square, deep in the intramural fabric (reverse geocode confirms Plaza del Ayuntamiento); the whole disc plus buffer lies inside the old city.
- **vezelay** (Vézelay): Single-street ridge burg accreted below the Benedictine abbey founded c. 858-9 by Girart de Roussillon and relocated to the defensible hilltop during the Norman raids of the 870s-880s. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination 84, Vézelay, Church and Hill (1979); abbey foundation acts in Monumenta Vizeliacensia (ed. Huygens).* Centre: On Rue Saint-Pierre, the single ridge street, ~190 m WSW of the basilica facade so the disc holds the whole burg (verification snapped the pre-registered point ~40 m south onto the spine; it had landed on the parallel lane Rue des Écoles); contamination note: low-density vineyard slopes enter the disc edges (Lavenham-scale settlement).

### planted pre-modern grid — origins and study centres

- **aigues-mortes** (Aigues-Mortes): Port new town founded by Louis IX on land bought from the abbey of Psalmodi (from 1240), granted a charter of franchises with consulate in May 1246, laid out on a quasi-regular grid later walled under Philip III. *Source: Charter of franchises of Louis IX, May 1246; Centre des monuments nationaux dossier, Tours et remparts d'Aigues-Mortes.* Centre: Place Saint-Louis, the market square inside the walled grid; the rectangular enceinte (~550x300 m) bounds the disc's fabric.
- **antigua-guatemala** (Antigua Guatemala): Santiago de Guatemala re-founded in the Panchoy valley in 1543 (cabildo installed 10 March 1543) after the 1541 destruction of the Almolonga site, laid out on the Spanish-colonial plaza-centred grid. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 65, Antigua Guatemala (1979); V. L. Annis, The Architecture of Antigua Guatemala 1543-1773 (1968).* Centre: Plaza Mayor, the founding plaza of the 1543 traza (point verified on the Fuente de las Sirenas at the plaza's centre); the colonial grid extends far beyond the disc in all directions.
- **beaumont-du-perigord** (Beaumont-du-Périgord): English bastide founded 1272 by Lucas de Thaney, seneschal of Guyenne for Edward I, with a royal charter of 15 November 1286. *Source: Charter of Edward I, 15 November 1286; Beresford, New Towns of the Middle Ages (1967); Fédération des Bastides d'Aquitaine inventory notice for Beaumont-du-Périgord.* Centre: Place Jean Moulin, the cornières square at the centre of the English bastide; the fortified church sits one block off, inside the disc.
- **bern** (Bern): Founded 1191 by Duke Berthold V of Zähringen as a planned town on the Aare peninsula, laid out in the characteristic Zähringer parallel-street scheme. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 267, Old City of Berne (1983); Historisches Lexikon der Schweiz, art. 'Bern (Gemeinde)'.* Centre: Kramgasse at the Zahringerbrunnen, the central axis of the 1191 Zahringer foundation on the Aare peninsula; the disc reads the medieval planned town and its 13th-century westward extension. Zahringer plan is parallel-street, not orthogonal-grid.
- **castelfranco-di-sopra** (Castelfranco di Sopra): Founded 1299 by decree of the Republic of Florence as one of the Valdarno terre nuove, on an orthogonal modular plan. *Source: D. Friedman, Florentine New Towns: Urban Design in the Late Middle Ages (MIT Press, 1988).* Centre: Piazza Vittorio Emanuele II, the central square of the 1299 plan; the village is small so the disc reaches surrounding open land, which dilutes but does not contaminate.
- **christiansfeld** (Christiansfeld): Founded 1773 by the Moravian Church under a concession from King Christian VII of Denmark, built to a regular plan on Lindegade/Norregade from 1773 to 1812. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 1468, Christiansfeld, a Moravian Church Settlement (2015), documenting the 1773 royal concession and plan.* Centre: Grev Zinzendorfs Plads, the church square at the heart of the Moravian grid; the founded core (~300x400 m) is smaller than the disc, so later Christiansfeld enters at the edge.
- **conwy** (Conwy): Bastide-type borough planted by Edward I with the castle in 1283, charter of liberties granted 8 September 1284. *Source: Charter of Edward I, 8 September 1284 (Calendar of Charter Rolls); Beresford, New Towns of the Middle Ages (1967); UNESCO WHL No. 374.* Centre: Lancaster Square, the market place at the head of High Street inside the town walls (square verified 11 m from centre; Nominatim's 'Conwy Marina Village' suburb label is a polygon artifact); the walled circuit is ~400 m across, so the disc reaches the estuary and castle — edge water thins fabric but adds no wrong-class fabric.
- **freudenstadt** (Freudenstadt): Founded 22 March 1599 by Duke Friedrich I of Württemberg, staked out by his architect Heinrich Schickhardt on a nine-men's-morris-board plan around Germany's largest market square. *Source: Foundation of 22 March 1599 documented in LEO-BW Ortslexikon (Landesarchiv Baden-Württemberg), Freudenstadt; Schickhardt's plans in the Hauptstaatsarchiv Stuttgart.* Centre: Centroid of the 219x216 m Marktplatz, the centre of Schickhardt's concentric-square plan (point verified interior to the square; Nominatim's 'Stadtbahnhof' label is a quarter-polygon artifact); buildings are post-1945 reconstructions on the 1599 plan (admissible per 0012 §5.3 — street/plot evidence, buildings context).
- **gluckstadt** (Glückstadt): Founded by King Christian IV of Denmark, who granted the new town its name, charter and arms on 22 March 1617, planned as a fortified Elbe port to rival Hamburg. *Source: Town charter of Christian IV, 22 March 1617 (town archives; documented in the official Glückstadt town history).* Centre: Am Markt, the hexagonal central market from which the radial-grid streets of the 1617 plan run to the former bastions ~250 m out; plan is radial-grid rather than orthogonal.
- **grenade-sur-garonne** (Grenade-sur-Garonne): Bastide founded by paréage of 4 September 1290 between Pierre Alfarici, abbot of Grandselve, and Eustache de Beaumarchais for Philip IV, with a customs charter of 1291. *Source: Paréage of 4 September 1290 and 1291 customs charter; Inventaire général du patrimoine culturel, Région Occitanie, notice IA31011135 (village dit bastide, Grenade).* Centre: The medieval market hall block at the centre of the 1290 grid, one of the most regular orthogonal bastide plans; town extends beyond the disc on all sides.
- **krakow** (Kraków): Chartered 5 June 1257 by Duke Bolesław V the Chaste under Magdeburg law, with the surveyed great market square and orthogonal street net laid out at foundation. *Source: Location charter of 5 June 1257, printed in Kodeks dyplomatyczny miasta Krakowa (ed. F. Piekosiński, 1879); UNESCO WHL No. 29.* Centre: Centre of Rynek Glowny, the 200 m market square of the 1257 charter grid; the 300 m disc stays well within the Planty ring, all charter-plan fabric.
- **marciac** (Marciac): Royal bastide founded by paréage of 1 August 1298 between Guichard de Marciac, seneschal of Toulouse, the abbot of La Case-Dieu and the count of Pardiac; customs confirmed by Philip IV in 1300. *Source: Paréage of 1 August 1298; Inventaire général du patrimoine culturel, Région Occitanie, notice IA32000172 (ville dite bastide, Marciac).* Centre: Place de l'Hôtel de Ville, the outsized central square of the 1298 bastide, still the organising element of the small town.
- **mirande** (Mirande): Bastide founded by paréage act of 27 May 1281 between Eustache de Beaumarchais (seneschal of Toulouse for the king of France), Count Bernard IV of Astarac and the Cistercian abbot of Berdoues. *Source: Paréage act of 27 May 1281; C. Higounet, 'Eustache de Beaumarchais et les bastides de Gascogne'; Mirande, monographie d'une bastide (Auch, 1981).* Centre: Place d'Astarac, the arcaded central square of the 1281 grid, which remains the town's dominant fabric.
- **monflanquin** (Monflanquin): Bastide founded 1256 by Alphonse de Poitiers, count of Toulouse and brother of Louis IX, with its charte de coutumes of 1256. *Source: Charte de coutumes of Monflanquin (1256); Beresford, New Towns of the Middle Ages (1967); Lauret, Malebranche & Séraphin, Bastides: villes nouvelles du Moyen Âge (1988).* Centre: Place des Arcades, the arcaded central square of the bastide; the hilltop core is ~300 m across, so the buffer takes in the modern fringe.
- **neuf-brisach** (Neuf-Brisach): New fortress town ordered by Louis XIV in 1698 after the loss of Breisach at Ryswick, designed and laid out by Vauban on a regular grid within an octagonal enceinte. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 1283, Fortifications of Vauban (2008); Vauban's 1698 project drawings (Service historique de la Défense).* Centre: Place d'Armes at the exact geometric centre of Vauban's octagonal grid; the entire fortified town fits inside the buffered disc.
- **palmanova** (Palmanova): Founded by decree of the Venetian Senate on 7 October 1593 as the fortress town of Palma, laid out as a nine-pointed star with radial-concentric streets. *Source: Venetian Senate foundation decree of 7 October 1593, cited in UNESCO WHL nomination No. 1533, Venetian Works of Defence (2017).* Centre: Piazza Grande, the hexagonal central place of the radial-surveyed 1593 plan; the whole nine-bastion town lies within ~500 m of it. Plan is radial, not orthogonal — included because the class criterion is a surveyed founded plan.
- **richelieu** (Richelieu): New town authorized by letters patent of Louis XIII in 1631 for Cardinal Richelieu — a town enclosed by walls and ditches with a market hall — built 1631-1642 to Jacques Lemercier's regular plan. *Source: Letters patent of Louis XIII, 1631 (incl. letters of October 1631); France Mémoire dossier 'La cité idéale du duc de Richelieu'.* Centre: Place du Marché, the southern of Lemercier's two squares, deep inside the walled rectangle of the 1631 plan.
- **sabbioneta** (Sabbioneta): Built 1554/1556-1591 by Vespasiano Gonzaga Colonna as a new fortified capital on an orthogonal surveyed plan within a star enceinte. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 1287, Mantua and Sabbioneta (2008), documenting Vespasiano Gonzaga's foundation.* Centre: Piazza Ducale, the civic square of the planned ducal capital; the walled town (~650x400 m) dominates the disc.
- **salisbury** (Salisbury, Wiltshire): New city founded 1219-1220 by Bishop Richard Poore under papal bull of Honorius III of 29 March 1219, laid out in rectangular chequers with standard 7x3-perch plots (bishop's charter 1225, royal charter of Henry III 1227). *Source: Papal bull of 29 March 1219 and royal charter of 1227, documented in VCH Wiltshire vol. 6 and RCHME, Ancient and Historical Monuments in the City of Salisbury (1980).* Centre: Market Place at the centre of the chequers grid of New Sarum; the Cathedral Close, a different fabric, enters only the disc's southern margin.
- **san-giovanni-valdarno** (San Giovanni Valdarno): Castel San Giovanni founded 1299 by decree of the Republic of Florence as a terra nuova in the upper Valdarno, its modular plan traditionally attributed to Arnolfo di Cambio. *Source: D. Friedman, Florentine New Towns: Urban Design in the Late Middle Ages (MIT Press, 1988).* Centre: Piazza Masaccio beside Palazzo d'Arnolfo, the central square pair of the 1299 terra nuova; 19th-20th-century industrial fringe enters the disc's edge.
- **scarperia** (Scarperia): Castel San Barnaba (Scarperia) founded 8 September 1306 by the Republic of Florence to control the Mugello road over the Apennines, laid out as an axial gridded terra nuova. *Source: D. Friedman, Florentine New Towns: Urban Design in the Late Middle Ages (MIT Press, 1988).* Centre: Palazzo dei Vicari fronting the central piazza of the 1306 plan; the planted borgo along the axis street dominates the disc.
- **valletta** (Valletta): Founded by the Order of St John, foundation stone laid 28 March 1566 by Grand Master Jean de Valette, gridiron plan by Francesco Laparelli continued by Girolamo Cassar. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 131, City of Valletta (1980), citing the 1566 foundation and Laparelli's plan.* Centre: Misrah San Gorg (Palace Square) on Republic Street, mid-peninsula and deep inside Laparelli's grid; peninsula edges enter only the buffer.
- **villa-de-leyva** (Villa de Leyva): Founded 12 June 1572 by Captain Hernán Suárez de Villalobos on the order of Andrés Díaz Venero de Leyva, president of the Nuevo Reino de Granada; relocated and re-founded on the present plaza-centred grid site by act of 10 May 1582, so the fabric at the centre dates from the 1582 act. *Source: Foundation act of 12 June 1572 and re-foundation act of 10 May 1582 (Archivo General de la Nación, Colombia); Decreto 3641 de 1954 declaring Villa de Leyva a National Monument.* Centre: Plaza Mayor, the vast founding plaza of the Indies-grid plan (point verified 4 m from the plaza object); the small town's edge comes within the buffer on the south and west.
- **villereal** (Villeréal): Bastide founded 1265 by Alphonse de Poitiers; its charte de coutumes of c. 1269 (original lost, Latin text preserved in the Gascon Rolls) was confirmed by Edward I on 1 May 1289. *Source: Charte de coutumes of Villeréal (c. 1269; Gascon Rolls, confirmation by Edward I, 1 May 1289); Beresford, New Towns of the Middle Ages (1967); Lauret, Malebranche & Séraphin, Bastides (1988).* Centre: Place de la Halle around the medieval market hall at the bastide's centre; core ~300 m across, modern fringe in the buffer.
- **winchelsea** (Winchelsea): New Winchelsea planned on Iham hill for Edward I from 1283 (commission to Stephen of Pencester, Gregory of Rokesle and Henry le Waleys) and settled by 1288-1292; the 1292 rental records its 39 surveyed quarters. *Source: D. Martin & B. Martin, New Winchelsea Sussex: A Medieval Port Town (2004); Beresford, New Towns of the Middle Ages (1967); the 1292 rental.* Centre: St Thomas's Street beside the parish church, in the central quarters of Edward I's grid; several quarters were never fully built or were later abandoned, so the disc reads a sparse but authentic planted grid.
- **zamosc** (Zamość): Founded by location act of 1580 issued by Chancellor Jan Zamoyski, designed as an ideal town by Bernardo Morando. *Source: UNESCO WHL nomination No. 564, Old City of Zamość (1992), which cites the 1580 foundation act of Jan Zamoyski.* Centre: Rynek Wielki, the central market square of Bernardo Morando's 1580 plan; the disc stays inside the fortified Renaissance town.

### 19th-century survey plat — origins and study centres

- **burra-sa** (Burra (Kooringa), South Australia): Company town of Kooringa laid out 1846 by George Strickland Kingston for the South Australian Mining Association — Australia's first company town and first surveyed mining township, serving the Burra Burra copper mine. *Source: Ian Auhl, The Story of the 'Monster Mine': The Burra Burra Mine and its Townships 1845-1877 (1986); Burra State Heritage Area declaration (28 January 1993).* Centre: Market Square, Kooringa — the square at the centre of Kingston's 1846 company plat (reverse-verified to Market Street frontage), town hall on its west side.
- **cheyenne-wy** (Cheyenne, Wyoming): Platted July 1867 by Gen. Grenville M. Dodge, Union Pacific chief engineer, as the railroad's Crow Creek division point; track arrived 13 November 1867. *Source: Downtown Cheyenne Historic District NRHP nomination (1978); G. M. Dodge, How We Built the Union Pacific Railway (1910).* Centre: Capitol Avenue at Lincolnway (16th Street), the Majestic/Hynds building corner of Dodge's 1867 plat between the UP depot and the later capitol — address-anchored at 1601-1602 Capitol Avenue (submitted point was ~170 m east); the capitol itself is ~1 km north, outside the disc, so the contamination risk is mid-rise redevelopment inside the core.
- **ellsworth-ks** (Ellsworth, Kansas): Townsite surveyed and platted 23 January 1867 on the edge of the Fort Ellsworth military reservation (plat filed 8 May 1867), the Kansas Pacific running its first cars through on 5 July 1867. *Source: Ellsworth Downtown Historic District NRHP nomination #07001065 (2007); A. T. Andreas / W. G. Cutler, History of the State of Kansas (1883), Ellsworth County.* Centre: North Douglas Avenue at North Main Street, the Insurance Building corner (115 N Douglas Avenue) at the centre of the NRHP downtown district (N Main to 3rd, Lincoln to Kansas Avenues) of the 1867 plat — submitted point was a house at 400 N Lincoln Avenue, ~330 m northeast, and was relocated.
- **emporia-ks** (Emporia, Kansas): Founded February 1857 by the five-member Emporia Town Company (including G. W. Brown and Preston B. Plumb), its speculative grid platted that spring between the Neosho and Cottonwood rivers. *Source: Laura M. French, History of Emporia and Lyon County (1929); Cutler/Andreas, History of the State of Kansas (1883), Lyon County.* Centre: Commercial Street 600 block, heart of the 1857 town company plat (reverse-verified to 602 Commercial Street).
- **ephraim-ut** (Ephraim, Utah): Settled from 1854 around Fort Ephraim (begun 7 February 1854) by families from Manti, the town extending from the fort on the standard Plat-of-Zion grid; Sanpete's principal fort until 1868. *Source: 'Ephraim', Utah History Encyclopedia (ed. A. K. Powell, 1994).* Centre: Main Street at 100 South (reverse-verified to 105 100 South), one block south of the co-op corner, keeping Snow College's modern campus at the disc edge.
- **greeley-co** (Greeley, Colorado): Founded March 1870 as the Union Colony, organized by Nathan Meeker with Horace Greeley's backing, its town platted by colony surveyors that spring. *Source: First Annual Report of the Union Colony of Colorado (1871); David Boyd, A History: Greeley and the Union Colony of Colorado (1890).* Centre: 8th Avenue at 9th Street, the commercial heart of Meeker's original Union Colony plat (reverse-verified onto 8th Avenue).
- **guthrie-ok** (Guthrie, Oklahoma): Created in a day in the federal Land Run of 22 April 1889 on a pre-designated 320-acre government townsite beside the Santa Fe line, laid out in a 300-ft-block cardinal grid; territorial and first state capital. *Source: Guthrie Historic District National Historic Landmark nomination (NPS, designated 1999; HABS OK-131).* Centre: Oklahoma Avenue at 1st Street, mid-block of the 1889 commercial grid inside the NHL district (reverse-verified to 141 S 1st Street, half a block off the corner); disc spans 2nd Street to Division and Cleveland to Harrison Avenues.
- **hastings-ne** (Hastings, Nebraska): Town plat filed 15 October 1872 at the crossing of the Burlington & Missouri River and St. Joseph & Denver City railroads on Walter Micklem's homestead, incorporated 1874, and made Adams County seat in 1878. *Source: A. T. Andreas, History of the State of Nebraska (1882), Adams County; Adams County Historical Society, 'Prairie to Prominence: Hastings' First 10 Years'.* Centre: 2nd Street at Hastings Avenue, the commercial centre of the rail-crossing plat (reverse-verified to The Kensington, 233 N Hastings Avenue, half a block north of the corner).
- **hays-ks** (Hays, Kansas): Platted 1867 by W. E. Webb's Big Creek Land Company, the Kansas Pacific's townsite agency, beside the new Fort Hays, superseding Cody and Rose's rival townsite of Rome after the railroad backed Hays City. *Source: Chestnut Street Historic District NRHP nomination (listed 2008, boundary increases 2011 and 2022); Cutler/Andreas, History of the State of Kansas (1883), Ellis County.* Centre: Main Street (former Chestnut Street) 1000 block, mid-point of the Chestnut Street NRHP district (reverse-verified to the Hays Arts Center at 11th Street).
- **indian-head-sk** (Indian Head, Saskatchewan): CPR main line arrived 1882; the townsite was secured and subdivided in 1883 by Major William Bell's Qu'Appelle Valley Farming Company, headquarters of the 53,000-acre Bell Farm. *Source: 'Indian Head', The Canadian Encyclopedia (Historica Canada); Bell Farm Historical Summary (Bell Barn Society of Indian Head).* Centre: Grand Avenue commercial blocks running north from the CPR station grounds (reverse-verified to Indian Head Town Hall, 421 Grand Avenue).
- **jamestown-sa** (Jamestown, South Australia): Government town surveyed 1871 in the Hundred of Belalie under Surveyor-General Goyder's department following the 1869 Wastelands Amendment Act, named for Governor Sir James Fergusson with streets named for Ayrshire towns. *Source: G. H. Manning, Manning's Place Names of South Australia (1990); Northern Areas Council township history (History of Jamestown).* Centre: Ayr Street main-street block at the town centre of the 1871 government survey (reverse-verified to 56 Ayr Street).
- **laramie-wy** (Laramie, Wyoming): Union Pacific townsite of spring 1868 — the railroad chose the site and sold 400 town lots in two weeks ahead of the May 1868 arrival of track. *Source: Laramie Downtown Historic District NRHP nomination #88002541 (listed 10 November 1988).* Centre: Ivinson Avenue at 2nd Street, centre of the NRHP downtown district facing the UP yard that created the town (reverse-verified to 205 S 2nd Street, Downtown Laramie); yard enters the disc's western edge.
- **lindsborg-ks** (Lindsborg, Kansas): Townsite laid out November 1869 in Section 17 by C. R. Carlson, John Ferm and A. Jacobson for the First Swedish Agricultural Company of Chicago, which had bought 13,160 acres in the Smoky Valley in 1868. *Source: Emory Lindquist, Smoky Valley People: A History of Lindsborg, Kansas (1953).* Centre: Main Street at Lincoln Street, centre of the company plat's commercial blocks (reverse-verified to 119 S Main Street).
- **longmont-co** (Longmont, Colorado): Platted March 1871 by Seth Terry for the Chicago-Colorado Colony (incorporated 20 November 1870), on 23,000 acres bought from the Denver Pacific's National Land Company. *Source: 'Chicago-Colorado Colony' and 'Longmont', Colorado Encyclopedia (Colorado Humanities).* Centre: Main Street between 4th and 5th Avenues, centre of the colony plat's commercial spine (reverse-verified to 412 Main Street, Longmont Downtown).
- **manti-ut** (Manti, Utah): Settled November 1849 by Isaac Morley's company at Brigham Young's direction; Jesse W. Fox surveyed the city plat on Plat-of-Zion principles in summer 1850, and Manti incorporated February 1851. *Source: 'Manti', Utah History Encyclopedia (ed. A. K. Powell, 1994); A. Antrei & A. Roberts, A History of Sanpete County (Utah Centennial County History Series, 1999).* Centre: Main Street at Union Street, the address-grid origin of Fox's 1850 plat (reverse-verified to 55 Main Street); county courthouse two blocks north, temple hill outside the disc.
- **maple-creek-sk** (Maple Creek, Saskatchewan): CPR townsite surveyed 1883 (survey arranged that August by CPR assistant land commissioner L. A. Hamilton) where construction crews had wintered in 1882-83, gaining the NWMP 'A' Division post transferred from Fort Walsh in 1883. *Source: 'Maple Creek', Encyclopedia of Saskatchewan (Canadian Plains Research Center, University of Regina, 2005).* Centre: Jasper Street 200-block commercial front south of the CPR line (post office at 201, shops at 217 Jasper Street) — submitted point sat ~350 m south in a residential stretch and was relocated.
- **newton-ks** (Newton, Kansas): AT&SF townsite of 1871 at the railhead reached in July 1871, succeeding Abilene as terminus of the Chisholm Trail cattle trade; Harvey County seat. *Source: Newton Main Street Historic District NRHP nomination (2003); Cutler/Andreas, History of the State of Kansas (1883), Harvey County.* Centre: Main Street 500 block at the AT&SF depot, core of the 1871 railroad plat (reverse-verified to 517 N Main Street).
- **norman-ok** (Norman, Oklahoma): Platted before the 22 April 1889 land run on the railway's townsite survey — the Seminole Town and Improvement Company, founded by Santa Fe railway officials, surveyed the station townsites — at the rail station established 1887, named for 1870s federal surveyor Abner E. Norman. *Source: 'Norman' (NO006) and 'Seminole Town and Improvement Company' (SE016), The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture (Oklahoma Historical Society).* Centre: 100 East Main Street, the first commercial block east of the Santa Fe tracks and zero point of the 1889 plat (forward-geocode of that address returns this exact point); depot grounds just across the tracks.
- **north-platte-ne** (North Platte, Nebraska): Union Pacific winter-terminus town platted late 1866 by the railroad's engineers under Grenville Dodge's location survey, briefly the archetypal Hell-on-Wheels camp before becoming a division point. *Source: A. T. Andreas, History of the State of Nebraska (1882), Lincoln County; G. M. Dodge, How We Built the Union Pacific Railway (1910).* Centre: Dewey Street 400 block, commercial core of the 1866 UP plat north of the yards (reverse-verified to 402 N Dewey Street).
- **panguitch-ut** (Panguitch, Utah): Settled March 1864 under Jens Nielson on a Mormon plat of sixty blocks in seven tiers, abandoned May 1866 in the Black Hawk War, permanently resettled March 1871. *Source: Panguitch Historic District NRHP nomination #06001068 (listed 16 November 2006).* Centre: Main and Center Street crossing of the 1864 plat (reverse-verified to 5 N Main Street), commercial row immediately north of the corner.
- **parowan-ut** (Parowan, Utah): Founded 13 January 1851 by George A. Smith's Iron Mission company as the Mormon mother settlement of southern Utah, laid out on the Plat-of-Zion grid. *Source: 'Parowan', Utah History Encyclopedia (ed. A. K. Powell, 1994).* Centre: Main and Center Street crossing of the 1851 plat, Old Rock Church corner (reverse-verified to 23 S Main Street).
- **perry-ok** (Perry, Oklahoma): Government townsite surveyed ahead of the Cherokee Outlet run of 16 September 1893, a tent city of tens of thousands by nightfall, platted around a courthouse square as Noble County seat. *Source: Perry Courthouse Square Historic District NRHP nomination #03000881 (listed 2 September 2003).* Centre: Noble County Courthouse on the central courthouse square of the 1893 government plat (reverse geocode lands on the courthouse itself).
- **quorn-sa** (Quorn, South Australia): Government town surveyed 1878 by Godfrey Walsh at the Great Northern Railway's Pichi Richi junction and proclaimed 16 May 1878, named for Quorndon, Leicestershire, within Governor Jervois's office. *Source: South Australian Government Gazette, vol. 23, pp. 1189-1190 (16 May 1878); R. Cockburn, Nomenclature of South Australia (1908).* Centre: Railway Terrace at the Transcontinental Hotel, the commercial front of the 1878 government plat facing the 1879 station — submitted point sat ~110 m southeast on residential First Street and was moved onto the terrace.
- **red-cloud-ne** (Red Cloud, Nebraska): Townsite of Silas Garber's colony platted 12 November 1872 as the seat of Webster County, its lots laid over Garber's homestead claim (filed 17 July 1870; county-seat vote 19 April 1871). *Source: A. T. Andreas, History of the State of Nebraska (1882), Webster County; Willa Cather Thematic Group NRHP multiple-property submission (1981-82).* Centre: Webster Street at 4th Avenue, the principal crossroads of the 1872 plat (reverse-verified to 313 N Webster Street); the Cather-era commercial row runs north from the corner.
- **salina-ks** (Salina, Kansas): Founded April 1858 by William A. Phillips's five-man town company from Lawrence, surveyed and platted by Phillips from March 1858 on a 320-acre site, chartered by the Sixth Territorial Legislature on 30 March 1859. *Source: Salina Town Company charter, Kansas Sixth Territorial Legislature (30 March 1859); Cutler/Andreas, History of the State of Kansas (1883), Saline County.* Centre: Santa Fe Avenue at Iron Avenue, the principal crossing of the original town company plat (reverse-verified to 108 S Santa Fe Avenue); urban-renewal-era side blocks are the noted contamination risk.
- **spring-city-ut** (Spring City, Utah): Settled by James Allred's company under Brigham Young's direction on 22 March 1852, driven out and permanently re-established in 1859 on a Plat-of-Zion grid; the entire town was designated an NRHP historic district in 1980. *Source: Spring City Historic District NRHP nomination (listed 22 October 1980; Utah SHPO).* Centre: Main and Center Street crossing, the Plat-of-Zion grid origin of the whole-town historic district (reverse-verified to 79 Main Street).
- **virden-mb** (Virden, Manitoba): Canadian Pacific Railway main-line townsite surveyed 1882 (early names Gopher Creek and Manchester, for the CPR-director Duke of Manchester), renamed Virden in 1883 after the Duke's country estate. *Source: Manitoba Historical Society, Manitoba Communities: Virden (Town); Virden CPR Station provincial heritage designation (Manitoba Heritage Site P079).* Centre: Nelson Street West store-front spine immediately west of the CPR line, at the named street's midpoint with the Wellington Street courthouse block adjacent — street-geometry anchor only (no address point obtainable), ~150 m uncertainty.

### mid-20th-century suburb — origins and study centres

- **bowie-belair** (Belair at Bowie, Maryland): Levitt & Sons' fourth mega-community, announced December 1957 and built 1960-ca.1970 on the Belair estate, first occupied October 1961. *Source: Historic American Buildings Survey, HABS MD-1253, Belair at Bowie, Maryland (Library of Congress item md1757, documented 2008).* Centre: interior of the Somerset section (S-streets around Somerset Park), the original section whose first six families arrived 17 October 1961; disc spills only into Buckingham, the second Levitt section. Verified: reverse geocode resolves to Stem Lane (an S-street), Bowie..
- **broadmeadows-dallas** (Dallas (Broadmeadows estate), Victoria): The Housing Commission of Victoria began its vast Broadmeadows estate in 1953; the Dallas neighbourhood was mass-built in the late 1950s-early 1960s. *Source: Renate Howe (ed.), New Houses for Old: Fifty Years of Public Housing in Victoria 1938-1988 (Ministry of Housing and Construction, 1988).* Centre: interior of the Housing Commission fabric named for Victorian towns (reverse geocode: Moe Court, Dallas); school campus and Merlynston Creek reserve held at the disc edge.
- **clairemont** (Clairemont, San Diego, California): Carlos Tavares and Lou Burgener launched Clairemont, the 'Village within a City', in 1950 as San Diego's first large postwar planned suburb. *Source: City of San Diego, San Diego Modernism Historic Context Statement (2007).* Centre: interior of the earliest units' Indian-named streets south-west of Balboa Avenue along Clairemont Drive (reverse geocode: Mohican Avenue, Clairemont); arterials and canyon outside the disc.
- **collier-heights** (Collier Heights, Atlanta, Georgia): A postwar suburb developed in the 1950s-60s largely by and for Atlanta's African American middle class - a nationally rare Black-developed tract suburb. *Source: NRHP nomination, Collier Heights Historic District (2009); Andrew Wiese, Places of Their Own (U. Chicago Press, 2004).* Centre: interior of the curvilinear ranch-house fabric on Baker Ridge Drive NW, inside the NRHP district; Collier Drive and I-20 outside the disc. Verified: reverse geocode resolves to Baker Ridge Drive Northwest, Atlanta..
- **curtin-act** (Curtin, Canberra): The National Capital Development Commission gazetted Curtin on 20 September 1962 and developed it 1963-66 as the second Woden Valley suburb of detached houses; first residents moved in during 1964. *Source: National Capital Development Commission Annual Reports (1963-66); ACT suburb gazettal records (gazetted 20 September 1962).* Centre: interior of the western residential loops off the Carruthers Street loop (reverse geocode: Nelson Place, Curtin, among the Ayers/Argyle Places); the group centre, schools and Yarra Glen parkway all outside the disc.
- **don-mills** (Don Mills, Toronto): E. P. Taylor's Don Mills Development Ltd announced the Macklin Hancock-planned community on 11 March 1953; the first house rose on Jocelyn Crescent that May. *Source: John Sewell, The Shape of the City: Toronto Struggles with Modern Planning (U. Toronto Press, 1993); Don Mills Residents Inc. history record of the first house (8 May 1953).* Centre: interior of the north-west quadrant first neighbourhood: the centre sits between Norden Crescent (~190 m) and Jocelyn Crescent (~250 m), both inside the disc (Nominatim-verified), with Lawrence Avenue at the disc's south edge and the quadrant's planned school/greenbelt at its north edge - both parts of Hancock's neighbourhood-unit plan..
- **elizabeth-sa** (Elizabeth South, South Australia): The South Australian Housing Trust began its satellite town in 1955; the first tenant, R. L. Pinnegar, moved into 10 Bubner Street, Elizabeth South, that November. *Source: Mark Peel, Good Times, Hard Times: The Past and the Future in Elizabeth (Melbourne UP, 1995); State Library of South Australia photograph B 62137 (first Housing Trust tenant).* Centre: interior of the first neighbourhood around Bubner Street (~130 m north-east of centre, Nominatim-verified), where the Trust's first tenant took occupancy in November 1955; Gawler rail line and Philip Highway held at or outside the disc edge..
- **elk-grove-village** (Elk Grove Village, Illinois): Centex Construction Company of Dallas master-planned and began building the village in 1956, pairing mass housing with a planned industrial park. *Source: Encyclopedia of Chicago (Chicago History Museum/Newberry Library, 2004), 'Elk Grove Village, IL'.* Centre: interior of the earliest Centex residential sections (reverse geocode: 342 Clearmont Drive, Elk Grove Village), north of the planned industrial park and away from arterial edges.
- **fraserview** (Fraserview, Vancouver): Central Mortgage and Housing Corporation expropriated 182 ha and built ~1,100 veterans' rental houses at Fraserview, begun in the late 1940s and completed in the early 1950s - Vancouver's largest federal housing project of the era. *Source: Jill Wade, Houses for All: The Struggle for Social Housing in Vancouver, 1919-50 (UBC Press, 1994); Vancouver Heritage Foundation, Places That Matter: Fraserview Veterans Housing.* Centre: interior of the CMHC scheme's north-east arm on Shawnee Place - the scheme's streets are named after golf courses of the world, confirming the fabric even though OSM's neighbourhood label at this point is Killarney (the 182 ha tract runs east of the official Victoria-Fraserview boundary); contamination note: roughly the southern quarter of the disc is Fraserview Golf Course, which the scheme wraps around.
- **green-valley-nsw** (Green Valley, Sydney): The Housing Commission of New South Wales turned the first sod of the Green Valley estate on 3 August 1961; 7,464 cottages, flats and units housed over 24,000 people by 1966 - the Commission's largest single scheme to that date. *Source: Housing Commission of New South Wales Annual Reports (1961-66); Liverpool City Library local history, 'Green Valley'.* Centre: interior of the cul-de-sac fabric south-west of the suburb centre (reverse geocode: Coquet Way, Green Valley); Wilson Road at the disc's east edge, school and plaza outside.
- **lake-hills** (Lake Hills, Bellevue, Washington): Developer R. H. (Dick) Connor, with builders Bell & Valdez and Leonard Homes, opened the 1,200-acre Lake Hills in 1955 on the Levittown model - the Pacific Northwest's first planned residential community of its scale; Bell & Valdez went on to build ~15,000 Eastside homes. *Source: HistoryLink.org (online encyclopedia of Washington state history), essays on Bellevue's Lake Hills neighborhood.* Centre: interior of the uniform tract fabric south of Main Street (reverse geocode: 159th Avenue Southeast, Lake Hills); shopping strip and church-school block outside the ring.
- **lakewood-ca** (Lakewood, California): Lakewood Park Company (Louis Boyar, Mark Taper, Ben Weingart) built 17,500 houses 1950-1953 at rates up to 100 houses a day. *Source: D. J. Waldie, Holy Land: A Suburban Memoir (W. W. Norton, 1996).* Centre: interior of the long north-south tract streets (Tanglewood/Ocana/Eastbrook) north of Centralia Street, clear of the Lakewood Center commercial strip, Carson Street and the high school. Verified: reverse geocode resolves to Ocana Avenue, Lakewood..
- **levittown-pa** (Levittown, Pennsylvania): Levitt & Sons built 17,311 houses 1952-1958 on assembly-line production, the second and largest Levittown. *Source: Dianne Harris (ed.), Second Suburb: Levittown, Pennsylvania (University of Pittsburgh Press, 2010).* Centre: interior of the Red Cedar Hill section between New Falls Road and Mill Creek Parkway; the 300 m disc lies wholly in 1952-58 Levitt fabric, with adjacent letter-sections (also Levitt) at the edges. Verified: reverse geocode resolves to Redbrook Lane, Red Cedar Hill, Levittown..
- **levittown-pr** (Levittown, Toa Baja, Puerto Rico): Levitt & Sons of Puerto Rico, Inc. opened sales on 5 September 1963 with exhibit homes dedicated by Governor Luis Munoz Marin; over 10,000 homes followed in eight sections - the firm's first project outside the continental US. *Source: Opening (5 September 1963) documented in contemporary press and the LevittownBeyond documentation project (levittownbeyond.com, Levittown PR).* Centre: interior of the western first-phase fabric beside the artificial lake ('Levittown Lakes'), south of Avenida Trio Los Andinos (reverse geocode: Calle Rosa de Tejas, Levittown, Toa Baja).
- **maryvale** (Maryvale, Phoenix, Arizona): Merchant builder John F. Long opened Maryvale in 1955 on a Victor Gruen Associates master plan - Arizona's first master-planned community, built at up to 20 houses a day with 125 sales a week by 1956. *Source: Philip VanderMeer, Desert Visions and the Making of Phoenix, 1860-2009 (U. New Mexico Press, 2010).* Centre: interior of the curvilinear superblock between 55th/59th Avenues and Campbell/Indian School - first-phase Long fabric (reverse geocode: 5642 W Glenrosa Avenue, Maryvale); the disc fits wholly inside the superblock, with the Maryvale Parkway civic spine excluded.
- **medina-kwinana** (Medina (Kwinana), Western Australia): The State Housing Commission of WA built Medina from 1952 for BP refinery workers on the town plan of Margaret Feilman, commissioned 1952 - WA's first postwar new town, with 653 homes completed by 1955. *Source: Heritage Council of Western Australia, inHerit place record 'Medina Townsite'; State Housing Commission annual reports (1952-55).* Centre: interior of the first residential precinct: Wheelock Road (~30 m) and Nannup Street (~90 m) bracket the centre (Nominatim-verified), east of Medina Avenue; Medina Oval only grazes the disc's east edge.
- **naenae** (Naenae, Lower Hutt): New Zealand's Department of Housing Construction mass-built Naenae from the mid-1940s on Ernst Plischke's 1943-46 neighbourhood-unit community plan. *Source: Ben Schrader, We Call It Home: A History of State Housing in New Zealand (Reed, 2005).* Centre: interior of the state-housing fabric south-east of the Hillary Court group centre, centred on Treadwell Street (with Vogel and Everest streets in the disc); the group centre sits ~400 m north-west, outside the disc, Naenae Park at the south-east edge, and the small bowling-club green ~120 m north-west of centre inside the disc. Coordinates corrected in verification: the submitted point (-41.2005, 174.9491) fell on the Naenae Bowling Club itself. Naenae Road, internal to the estate, crosses the disc..
- **panorama-city** (Panorama City, Los Angeles, California): Kaiser Community Homes (Henry J. Kaiser and Fritz B. Burns) developed the Valley's first planned community from 1947-48 using factory-style site prefabrication. *Source: Greg Hise, Magnetic Los Angeles: Planning the Twentieth-Century Metropolis (Johns Hopkins UP, 1997).* Centre: mid-point of the original Kaiser Community Homes superblock bounded by Van Nuys Blvd, Roscoe Blvd, Woodman Ave and Chase St (reverse geocode: 8446 Colbath Avenue, inside the superblock); Roscoe arterial just outside the ring.
- **park-forest** (Park Forest, Illinois): American Community Builders (Philip Klutznick) began the planned GI town in 1948, with the single-family phases built 1950-1953. *Source: Gregory C. Randall, America's Original GI Town: Park Forest, Illinois (Johns Hopkins UP, 2000); W. H. Whyte, The Organization Man (1956).* Centre: interior of the early-1950s single-family neighbourhood (Mohawk/Mantua/Miami streets) west of Indianwood Boulevard, away from the 1948 rental courts and the Plaza; Sauk Trail kept outside the disc. Verified: reverse geocode resolves to Mantua Court, Park Forest..
- **prairie-village** (Prairie Village, Kansas): The J. C. Nichols Company platted Prairie Village in 1941 and mass-built it 1945-1955; it was named 'the best planned community in America' in 1949. *Source: William S. Worley, J.C. Nichols and the Shaping of Kansas City (U. Missouri Press, 1990).* Centre: interior of the Nichols Company fabric north-east of 75th and Nall (reverse geocode: Rosewood Drive, Prairie Village, in the winding postwar core); shopping village outside the disc.
- **san-lorenzo** (San Lorenzo Village, California): David D. Bohannon's mass-produced village began in 1944 as war-worker housing and continued through the late 1940s as a model merchant-builder operation; production dominated by the 1945-51 phases. *Source: Greg Hise, Magnetic Los Angeles (Johns Hopkins UP, 1997), on Bohannon's production building including San Lorenzo Village.* Centre: interior of the Via-named streets at the village core (reverse geocode: Via Coralla, San Lorenzo); golf course and Bohannon school pushed to the disc edge.
- **sharpstown** (Sharpstown, Houston, Texas): Frank W. Sharp opened his ~6,500-acre master-planned, air-conditioned tract community in 1955, among the largest in the US at the time. *Source: Handbook of Texas Online, 'Sharpstown, TX' (Texas State Historical Association).* Centre: interior of the earliest sections south of Bellaire Boulevard (reverse geocode: 6690 Stroud Drive, Sharpstown); Bayland Park and the elementary school held at the disc edge.
- **westlake-daly-city** (Westlake, Daly City, California): Henry Doelger began Westlake in 1949 and built roughly 6,500 dwellings there through the early 1960s - the archetypal 'little boxes' suburb. *Source: Rob Keil, Little Boxes: The Architecture of a Classic Midcentury Suburb (Advection Media, 2006).* Centre: interior of Doelger's earliest Westlake units south-west of the Westlake shopping centre (reverse geocode: Pinehaven Drive, Westlake), with Skyline Blvd and John Daly Blvd outside the disc.
- **wildwood-winnipeg** (Wildwood Park, Winnipeg): Contractor Hubert Bird (Bird Construction) built Wildwood Park in 1946-47 as Canada's first Radburn-plan suburb, to a Green Blankstein Russell site plan, with lettered bays facing a continuous interior park and houses prefabricated on site in five variants of three basic designs. *Source: Winnipeg Architecture Foundation, Wildwood Park documentation.* Centre: centre of the Radburn-plan bay system with its interior greens (reverse geocode: Wildwood Park Section I - one of the lettered bays) - the disc reads nearly the whole neighbourhood; Red River enters the disc's southern edge (peninsula site, unavoidable).
- **willingboro** (Willingboro (Levittown), New Jersey): Levitt & Sons' third Levittown, built 1958-1964 (Somerset Park opened 7 June 1958) and studied from within by sociologist Herbert Gans. *Source: Herbert J. Gans, The Levittowners: Ways of Life and Politics in a New Suburban Community (Pantheon, 1967); Somerset Park documentation at levittownbeyond.com.* Centre: on Sussex Drive in the interior of Somerset Park, the documented first section (opening day 7 June 1958; first family occupied 51 Sussex Drive in October 1958); Veterans Parkway, the Levitt-plan collector, crosses ~200 m east and US 130 lies ~450 m west, outside the disc. Coordinates corrected in verification: the submitted point (40.0417, -74.8991) reverse-geocoded to Sylvan Lane in the Charleston section, ~950 m from Somerset Park..

### contemporary informal — origins and study centres

- **baseco-manila** (BASECO Compound, Tondo, Manila): Informal settlement of port and shipyard workers and migrants self-built from the 1970s-80s (about 200 families by the mid-1980s, growing to tens of thousands) on reclaimed harbour land in the Bataan Shipyard & Engineering Co. compound; Presidential Proclamation 145 (18 January 2002) later declared the land open for disposition to its occupants. *Source: Presidential Proclamation No. 145 (s. 2002), Republic of the Philippines; UN-Habitat/PSA Tondo foreshore studies; Places Journal 'Mangroves on a Landfill' (2021).* Centre: south-western irregular fabric of the harbour-mouth peninsula (reverse-geocodes to BASECO Compound, Port Area), away from the NGO-built Gawad Kalinga/Habitat village in the north-east (~510 m); shoreline clips the disc's western and southern fringes (spit geometry, as at West Point); note the 2004 state landfill works expanded the peninsula — centre sits in the older self-built core.
- **bwaise-kampala** (Bwaise III, Kampala): Unplanned settlement that grew in the Nsooba/Lubigi wetland from the 1960s-70s as rural in-migration overflowed planned Kampala, self-built without drainage, planning, or tenure. *Source: ACTogether Uganda & National Slum Dwellers Federation of Uganda Kampala slum profiles (2014); HOT/KCCA Mapping and Surveying of the Nakamiro Channel Area (Open Cities Kampala); YouthMappers Bwaise-Kalerwe flood mapping (2019).* Centre: Jambula/Katale zone of Bwaise III wetland fabric (reverse-geocodes to the Katale place node), ~340 m north of the Northern Bypass and ~340 m west of the Bombo Road commercial frontage so the disc reads the flood-plain settlement interior.
- **canaan-pap** (Canaan, Croix-des-Bouquets): Self-built city that arose within weeks of the 22 March 2010 utilité-publique decree on the Canaan hills after the earthquake, settled by displaced families without state planning or tenure. *Source: UCLBP & UN-Habitat Canaan urban development initiative documentation (2015-16); Government of Haiti arrêté of 22 March 2010; American Red Cross 'Drones over Canaan' (2018; 35 km2 drone imagery to OSM, Dec 2017).* Centre: upper hill fabric north of 1ère Avenue Canaan in consolidated central Canaan (reverse-geocodes to Rue Saint-Vil, Canaan, 2ème Crochus), away from the Route Nationale 1 frontage; some sectors were gridded by informal land brokers at/after occupation (self-organized, not state-surveyed) — the chosen slope fabric is the organic accretion.
- **dharavi-mumbai** (Dharavi, Mumbai): Grew from the Koliwada fishing village and late-19th-century tannery and potter settlements into Mumbai's largest informal district, with its principal self-built expansion post-1950 and no formal planning or tenure. *Source: Sharma, K. (2000) Rediscovering Dharavi: Stories from Asia's Largest Slum, Penguin India.* Centre: Laxmi Baug/Social Nagar fabric mid-district, G/N Ward; centre snaps to 90 Feet Road, an internal Dharavi arterial that is nearer than the original 'southern edge' claim — disc remains wholly Dharavi fabric; the Sion rail corridor is ~540 m east; pre-1950 nucleus caveat applies — main self-built expansion is post-1950, matching pilot Kibera's profile.
- **dworzark-freetown** (Dworzark (Dwarzark), Freetown): Hillside settlement populated from the 1940s and self-built up the Freetown slopes in twelve sections without formal layout, expanding sharply through 1990s civil-war displacement. *Source: CODOHSAPA/FEDURP settlement profile of Dworzark; SLURC Community Area Action Plan documentation; Freetown citywide community drone mapping (CODOHSAPA/FEDURP/OSM Sierra Leone, HOT KYC.Africa).* Centre: at the mapped Dwarzark label (reverse-geocodes to Dwarzark, New England/Sumalia Town) with dense hillside fabric continuous >=300 m in all directions; centre is mid-slope, away from the New England institutional edge.
- **epworth-harare** (Epworth, Harare): Self-built settlement on Epworth Methodist Mission land from the 1970s-80s, densified by liberation-war and post-2005 (Murambatsvina) displacement, without planning or tenure at formation. *Source: Chitekwe-Biti, Mudimu, Nyama & Jera (2012) 'Developing an informal settlement upgrading protocol in Zimbabwe - the Epworth story', Environment & Urbanization 24(1).* Centre: Chinamano Extension fabric in south-eastern Epworth (reverse-geocodes to Chinamano Extension, Ward 5, Epworth), one of the informal 'extension' wards rather than the older mission village core; fabric continuous well beyond the disc.
- **heliopolis-sp** (Heliopolis, Sao Paulo): Occupation from 1971-72 of federal IAPAS land in Ipiranga by families removed from central Sao Paulo (trucked to unserviced land they built themselves), self-built and densified without planning; Sao Paulo's largest favela complex. *Source: Sampaio, M.R.A. (1991) Heliopolis: o percurso de uma invasao, FAU-USP; Prefeitura de Sao Paulo/SEHAB Heliopolis regularization documentation.* Centre: deep in the irregular street web south of the Nova Heliopolis label (reverse-geocodes to Rua Nova Heliopolis, Sacoma); Estrada das Lagrimas ~440 m west, the Sabesp site ~350 m east, Av.
