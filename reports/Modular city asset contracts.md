# City kits put meaning in placement

Studios build towns in a fixed order (2D map, roads and district types, greybox massing, kit buildings swapped in for the grey blocks, layered dressing), and in every sourced case the kit is hand-made even when a tool assembles it. No well-sourced shipped game generates its settlements end to end. The asset itself carries almost nothing: file formats standardise axes and units at best (only glTF fixes "front"), while pivot, module size, connection points and role are private conventions held in kit documentation, piece names or the tool's rule table. Procedural tools agree with that split: an asset supplies a bounding size and an agreed axis, and role, facing and stretch belong to the slot that places it. The pain studios report is not making pieces but changing them: a late pivot or footprint change touches hundreds of placed instances, and regenerating an upstream layer destroys hand edits unless those edits are stored as inputs or keyed overrides. For a text-first framework this is good news. Its existing rule (the user makes the model, the pack states facts, generated work lowers to explicit instances) matches practice, and the gap to fill is the convention-only layer: a written frame, a pivot rule, a module distinct from bounds, kit roles, connectors, and a contract diff when a piece is revised. Sections 1 to 3 are sourced findings; sections 4 to 6 are this report's reasoning from them; section 7 says which conclusions rest on thin evidence.

Evidence flags used throughout: **(summary)** means seen only in a search-result summary; **(secondary)** means third-party notes on a talk; **(recalled)** means stated from prior knowledge of a standard whose page could not be fetched; **(inference)** means the notes' or this report's reasoning. Pages were read through a tool that summarises them, so wording attributed to sources is paraphrase.

## 1. Studios lay roads first, greybox second, and swap in hand-made kits

The published workflows agree on the order of layers. Watch Dogs 2 went from research and story, to a 2D city map of gameplay locations, roads and district types, to a minimal greybox with gameplay metrics, to detailing, with the final layered detail pass in the last months ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/building-urban-playgrounds-for-video-games)). The Witcher 3 followed the same shape: story fixes dimensions and points of interest, grey boxes fix city size, design teams decide shop and inn counts, then meshes, decoration, paths and foliage, with optimisation last ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/world-building-of-witcher-3/)). Procedural pipelines formalise the order as a dependency chain: in Ghost Recon Wildlands each layer (terrain, rivers, roads, railways, forest, settlements) is the base for the next ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-technology-in-ghost-recon-wildlands/)). **Roads come before buildings everywhere and are splines carrying attributes**, which makes them the upstream dependency whose changes ripple furthest (inference).

Ownership splits four ways (inference from the Watch Dogs 2 description): level designers and level artists own layout; a content or kit team models the pieces; technical artists own the tool that binds pieces to layout; level artists pick the style per neighbourhood and own final dressing ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/building-urban-playgrounds-for-video-games)). In Watch Dogs 2 the building tool takes a top-down footprint, each edge spawns a "kit recipe", and road splines own their street furniture through recipes (street lights about every 15 m, bins at intersections), so moving the spline moves its props ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/building-urban-playgrounds-for-video-games)). No source describes an explicit lot or parcel layer in a shipped game; the unit below the block is a footprint shape or a street edge.

### What a kit artist delivers is a frozen contract, then geometry

Bethesda's two talks are the only sources found with explicit numbers. A kit is a set of snapping pieces built to one agreed footprint: a 512-unit room tiles with a 256-unit hallway, a 384-unit room eventually creates gaps, and sub-kit footprints must be multiples of each other ([GDC 2013 transcript](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/skyrim-s-modular-approach-to-level-design)). The footprint is the full claimed bounds, not the walkable space, and pieces should not build to its edge. Pivots sit at the ground plane and centre, with documented exceptions; the Fallout 4 talk's rule is "Pick a pivot, and stick with it" ([GDC 2016 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/JoelBurgess/gdc-2016-modular-level-design-of-fallout-4)). Names encode kit, sub-kit, piece type and a trailing two-digit visual variant, and the authors could not decode one token of their own example. Delivery is staged: a proof of near-zero-detail pieces (1 to 3 weeks) settles footprint, logic and naming; a graybox is stress-tested in real layouts; build-out and variants come last ([GDC 2013 transcript](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/skyrim-s-modular-approach-to-level-design)). Three acceptance tests recur: **loopback** (the kit closes on itself), **stack** (floors have thickness) and **gap** (enough glue pieces for off-angle work) ([The Level Design Book](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics/modular)). These contracts are published for interiors and dungeons; no piece list or module size was found for an exterior facade kit.

### The cost is in change, not in construction

Instance-based kits make geometry revisions free and contract revisions catastrophic: changing a pivot late can require manual updates to hundreds of instances ([GDC 2013 transcript](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/skyrim-s-modular-approach-to-level-design)). Art changes after design markup silently broke gameplay (cover became thin poles, a wall became see-through chain-link). In Wildlands manual edits were lost at the next procedural update unless the data was locked first ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-technology-in-ghost-recon-wildlands/)). Three strategies for hand work against regeneration are published: lock the region (Wildlands), keep regeneration to layers that carry no hand work (Far Cry 5, secondary), or re-validate hand work against rules and keep what still passes (Spider-Man). No source describes a versioning or migration mechanism for a revised kit piece across a built city.

## 2. Formats standardise axes and units; everything a kit needs is convention

| Aspect | Formally standardised | Convention only |
|---|---|---|
| Up axis and handedness | glTF: +Y up, right-handed, normative. OpenUSD: `upAxis` Y or Z per stage. FBX: declared per file. | Engines fix their own world: Unreal is left-handed Z-up, Godot right-handed Y-up |
| Unit | glTF: metres. OpenUSD: `metersPerUnit` per stage, falling back to centimetres. FBX: declared, centimetres by default. | Unreal works in centimetres; Unity and Godot expect 1 unit = 1 m |
| Front | **glTF only**: the front of an asset faces +Z. Godot documents the same rule. | USD defines none; FBX declares a front axis but its meaning is tool-dependent; Unreal's +X forward is a world convention |
| Pivot | Nothing in any format | Epic: build at the origin, typically a corner. Bethesda: ground-plane centre. Shared rules: on the ground, on the grid, one rule per kit, never changed after placement begins |
| Module or footprint size | Nothing; no format carries it | Bethesda 512 and 256 units with snap at half the footprint; Unreal kits on a 100 cm grid with 400 cm walls (summary). The one stated rule: sub-kit footprints are integer multiples |
| Connection points | Carriers only: Unreal sockets (imported from helpers named `SOCKET_…`), glTF nodes, USD prims of kind `subcomponent` | The meaning of any connection point; no typed connector vocabulary was found |
| Role (wall, corner, door) | Nothing | Tokens in piece names, or the slot a piece is assigned to in a tool |
| Metadata | USD: `kind`, `assetInfo`, `customData`, variant sets, purposes, extents hints. glTF: free-form `extras`, XMP provenance, material variants. 3D Tiles: typed property schemas | Any schema for kit facts; the standards give a place to store them, not a vocabulary |
| Collision and level of detail | Engine-reserved name tokens that differ per engine (`UCX_` in Unreal, `-col` in Godot, `_LOD0` in Unity (summary)) | No cross-engine naming; glTF physics is still draft |

Sources: [glTF 2.0](https://registry.khronos.org/glTF/specs/2.0/glTF-2.0.html), [USD up axis](https://openusd.org/release/api/group___usd_geom_up_axis__group.html), [USD units](https://openusd.org/release/api/group___usd_geom_linear_units__group.html), [FBX SDK](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2016/ENU/FBX-Developer-Help/files/GUID-CC93340E-C4A1-49EE-B048-E898F856CFBF.htm), [Unreal coordinates](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/coordinate-system-and-spaces-in-unreal-engine), [Unreal FBX static mesh pipeline](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/fbx-static-mesh-pipeline-in-unreal-engine), [Godot export considerations](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_3d_scenes/model_export_considerations.html), [Godot name suffixes](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_3d_scenes/node_type_customization.html), [OpenUSD glossary](https://openusd.org/release/glossary.html), [glTF extension registry](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/glTF/blob/main/extensions/README.md), [3D Tiles metadata](https://github.com/CesiumGS/3d-tiles/blob/main/specification/Metadata/README.adoc), [Allar style guide](https://github.com/Allar/ue5-style-guide).

Two consequences matter for a consumer. Names are a lossy channel, and engine-reserved tokens collide with free naming: a piece whose name ends in `_col` or starts with `UCX_` is reinterpreted on import (inference). And the important bound for a modular piece is the footprint it claims on the grid, which is generally not its mesh bounding box and is declared by no standard field.

### City structure has a shared vocabulary, except for block and lot

OpenStreetMap, CityGML/CityJSON, IFC, OpenDRIVE and the procedural-city literature repeat about a dozen nouns. Block and lot appear only in the procedural literature.

| Term | Working definition | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Street | A centreline with a class and a width or lane count; lanes are lateral offsets from the same line | OSM `highway=*` ways ([OSM](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Key:highway)); OpenDRIVE reference line ([ASAM](https://www.asam.net/standards/detail/opendrive/)) |
| Junction | Where street segments meet: a shared node in OSM and in Parish and Müller; a set of connecting roads in OpenDRIVE | Same sources; representations diverge most here |
| Block | A face enclosed by the street graph, inset by street width | Procedural literature only ([Parish and Müller 2001](https://cgl.ethz.ch/Downloads/Publications/Papers/2001/p_Par01.pdf)) |
| Lot | A subdivision cell of a block; lots without direct street access are discarded, so **frontage** is the validity rule | Parish and Müller; absent from OSM and CityJSON as read |
| Building | A footprint polygon with height or levels and a type | OSM `building=*` ([Simple 3D Buildings](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Simple_3D_Buildings)); CityJSON `Building` ([spec](https://www.cityjson.org/specs/2.0.1/)) |
| Building part | A volume inside the footprint with its own height and roof; when parts exist they replace the outline | OSM `building:part`; CityJSON `BuildingPart` |
| Storey | A count in OSM (`building:levels`, with `roof:levels` separate); an object in CityJSON (`BuildingStorey`) and the primary container in IFC (recalled) | Same sources |
| Roof | A shape from a short list (flat, gabled, hipped, mansard and others) plus height and ridge orientation; facade height is total height minus roof height | OSM Simple 3D Buildings |
| Opening | `Window` and `Door` surfaces within a `WallSurface` | CityJSON semantic surfaces; no OSM equivalent |

Use is described at three scales (zone, building type, occupant function) in OSM, and every map-to-game importer found reduces a building to footprint, height or levels, a type tag and optionally roof shape and colour ([Houdini Labs OSM Import](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/labs--osm_import.html); [StreetMap plugin](https://github.com/ue4plugins/StreetMap)). No documented game pipeline was found that consumes CityGML semantics, and none imports blocks or lots; richer structure is generated downstream.

## 3. Procedural tools ask almost nothing of the asset and keep rules in the placement

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CityEngine CGA | Text shape grammar: component split, subdivision, repeat, insert | Nothing declared; bounding box and up axis are used; default insert stretches the box to the scope | The placement: face normals classified against scope, object, world or street | Trim planes mitre geometry; or a rule splits off a corner strip (inference) | Rule-side: absolute, relative and floating sizes with repeat | Not verified |

Four patterns fall out of the table (inference). There are two families of contract: grammar and marker systems need only a bounding size and an axis convention and put role, facing and stretch in the rule; constraint and snap systems make the asset carry its own interface. Leftover length has three answers: repeat with a slight stretch of every piece, absorb the remainder in one filler or scalable piece, or quantise the world so no remainder exists. Corners have three answers: mitre the geometry, use dedicated convex and concave pieces, or make the corner the primitive. And hand edits survive only as edits to the generator's input, as keyed overrides, or by detaching output; no system examined was confirmed to merge free edits of generated output back in. No vendor-neutral standard for kit metadata or building grammars was found, and CityEngine ignores FBX custom attributes and reads only meshes from USD, so kit metadata does not travel inside the asset even where the container could hold it ([CGA insert](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/latest/cga/cga-i.htm)).

## 4. What this means: own the convention-only layer, leave the kit and the rules' content to the user

*This section is the report's reasoning from the findings above, read against the framework's own SPEC.md (sections 8 and 11) and FINDINGS.md. It is not sourced fact.*

The framework's central rule is confirmed by practice. A sidecar pack that states facts about a model the user made is what every tool effectively does (PCG's module table, Houdini's role slots, Dungeon Architect's theme bindings), and lowering to explicit instances is the same hand-off Houdini Engine makes to Unreal (asset reference, position, orientation, scale, grouping key). The framework should not move facts into the model file or into names. What it lacks is exactly the layer the standards leave open, and that layer is small.

**What the framework should own, agnostically.** First, the frame: it already reads binary glTF, and glTF is the one format that fixes up, front and unit, so "front" stops being an unwritten convention by adopting glTF's rule (+Y up, +Z front, metres) as written, with a per-asset correction for models that do not comply. Kit runs (`faces`), `flank`, `against` and `beside` all depend on it today. Second, the kit contract as checkable data: pivot rule, module per axis, footprint as distinct from bounds, roles, variants, connectors, surfaces and clearances. The college world already found that a bay measuring 6.44 m repeats at 6 m and that `module_m` was set by judgement; that is Bethesda's footprint-versus-bounds distinction rediscovered. Third, the checks: Bethesda's loopback, stack and gap tests and the existing `kit.misfit` are compiler diagnostics in waiting. Fourth, change accounting: studios' worst cost is a contract change to a placed piece, and no source describes tooling for it, so a diff of contract facts between pack revisions, listing affected instances, is the most distinctive thing the framework can offer. Fifth, the structural vocabulary of section 2 and the derived relations (junction, block, frontage). Sixth, stable identity and keyed overrides for lowered instances, with re-validation, since that is the only way hand work survives regeneration anywhere.

**Where each fact should come from.**

| Fact | Source | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Up, front, unit | Fixed by adopting glTF's rule; `front` correction declared per asset when needed | Only glTF standardises front |
| Bounds, triangle count, vertical lift | Measured (as today) | PCG also reads size from bounds |
| Origin position relative to bounds | Measured, then checked against a pivot rule declared once per kit (base centre, base corner, or explicit) | Pivot is convention everywhere; a mismatch should be a diagnostic, not a visual surprise |
| Module (run length, depth, storey height) and footprint | Declared; checked against measured bounds with a declared allowed overhang | No format carries it; bounds are not the module |
| Solidity, passable footprint, ground tolerance | Declared | Gameplay facts; the gatehouse case needed "neither solid nor empty" |
| Role, family, variant group | Declared in a kit table in the pack, not on the asset and never parsed from names | Role is a property of the slot in every grammar tool; names are lossy |
| Connectors (position, outward facing, type) | Declared in the pack; optionally read from named nodes in the model and compared | Named nodes are the one portable carrier, but names are not unique and no vocabulary exists |
| Named surfaces (a table top) and clearances (door swing, front access) | Declared; could also be read from named nodes | Needed by `dress` and by the interior checks |
| Allowed stretch | Declared per asset, default none | PCG's scalable flag; CGA stretches by default, which art does not always tolerate |
| Collision and level-of-detail meshes | Not in the contract; passed through for an engine adapter | Conventions differ per engine and collide by name |

Reading connectors and surfaces from named nodes is feasible without widening the loader, since node names and transforms live in the JSON chunk the compiler already reads. Treat node-read values as measured facts and the pack as the contract; when both exist, disagreement is a diagnostic.

**What stays the user's responsibility.** Making the model and the kit; choosing module size, storey height and pivot rule (the framework records and enforces the choice, it does not make it); authoring the kit's pieces in the staged order studios use; writing the content of recipes (which pieces a street edge or a building edge gets, which style a district uses); street layout and building footprints; and all engine-side concerns such as collision, level of detail and materials.

**What the framework should not attempt.** A two-dimensional facade grammar with geometry cutting in the manner of CGA: it requires mesh operations and breaks the rule that output is instances of user-made models. Constraint solving in the manner of Wave Function Collapse, and adjacency derived from mesh cross-sections. Generating street networks or subdividing lots: no studio source shows this in a shipped settlement, and the unit studios work with is a footprint or street edge. Adopting CityGML or IFC as a schema: borrow their terms, not their models. Merging free edits of generated output: no examined tool does it. Standardising names.

**Structural vocabulary to adopt.** Street (a path with class and width), junction (derived where streets meet), block (derived face of the street graph), building (footprint, levels, height, roof shape, use), storey (map it onto the existing level frames), roof, opening (already a point feature on an outline). Make **frontage** an explicit relation from a building to a street edge, and defer lot until a producer needs it. Rename the massing-level term: OSM and CityJSON call it "building part", which collides with the framework's own "part"; "wing" or "mass" avoids that. Keep use at three scales (zone, building type, occupant), measure height from ground to roof top with roof height separate, and resolve missing attributes in the order explicit value, then rule, then type default.

## 5. How the framework should evolve, in order

*This section is the report's reasoning, not sourced fact. Each change names the finding it answers.*

**Asset contract.**

1. Write the frame down. State glTF's +Y up, +Z front, metres as the pack's rule and add a per-asset `front` correction. Answers: only glTF standardises front; four producers rely on it unwritten.
2. Split module from bounds. Replace the one-axis `module_m` with a module per axis (run, depth, storey height) and an allowed overhang, and report measured bounds against it. Answers: Bethesda's footprint rule; the college's 6.44 m bay at a 6 m pitch.
3. Declare a pivot rule per kit and check it against the measured origin. Answers: pivot is pure convention, and a late pivot change is the costliest revision studios describe.
4. Add a kit table: family, shared module and pivot rule, roles bound to asset lists, and variant groups, with a check that variants in a group share footprint and pivot. Answers: role belongs to the slot; trailing variant numbers mean "interchangeable by construction".
5. Add connectors, named surfaces and clearances as declared facts, optionally read from named nodes. Include a passable footprint and a fallback for an unused connector (Dungeon Architect fills an unstitched doorway with wall). Answers: the gaps listed in FINDINGS (no surfaces, no clearances, arcades) and the absence of any connector standard.
6. Record a lock of each asset's contract facts and diff it on every compile. A geometry-only revision passes silently; a change to bounds, pivot, module or connectors lists every instance and run it affects. Answers: contract changes touch hundreds of instances, and no migration mechanism is documented anywhere.

**Assembly rules.**

7. Give a kit run an explicit remainder policy: fail and suggest closing lengths (today's behaviour), stretch within the declared tolerance, or insert a declared filler role. Answers: the three industry answers to leftover length.
8. Let a kit run follow a polyline or a footprint outline, with end, convex-corner and concave-corner roles, and stack by storey with ground and top-ledge roles. Answers: Watch Dogs 2 spawns a recipe per footprint edge; Houdini and Buildify use dedicated corner slots and per-floor slicing.
9. Add a named recipe: a one-dimensional pattern over roles (sequence, repeat, weighted choice) bound to a kit, lowering to instances. Stop at one dimension. Answers: "recipe" and "theme" are the studio terms for exactly this, and PCG's grammar shows one dimension plus stacking is enough for fences and facades.
10. Add keyed overrides on lowered instances, re-validated each compile: an override whose target vanished or now fails a check is reported, never dropped. Give scattered instances stable ids. Answers: hand edits survive only as inputs or keyed overrides; Spider-Man keeps hand work that still meets specification.
11. Ship kit self-tests as a command: loopback, stack and gap layouts generated from the kit table, with measured seams between neighbouring bays. Answers: Bethesda's acceptance tests; FINDINGS notes bay joins were never measured.

**City structure.**

12. Add street kinds with class and width, derived junctions, and street-owned dressing through recipes along the path and at junctions. Answers: roads are the upstream layer everywhere; Watch Dogs 2 binds furniture to the spline.
13. Derive blocks from the street graph and add a `fronts` relation on buildings, with checks for buildings off their frontage or inside a street. Answers: block and frontage are the relations the mapping standards omit and the procedural literature treats as validity rules.
14. Add a building record (footprint, levels, roof shape, use) that compiles as box massing with a greybox pack and as kit pieces with a real pack, from one source. Answers: every studio greyboxes first and swaps; every importer reduces a building to this record.
15. Allow rotation for parts and a building-scale set. Answers: Fallout 4's pack-ins are reusable assemblies of fine pieces, and parts today translate only.

## 6. Dogfood: a market-street district on a slope, built twice

*This section is the report's proposal, not sourced fact.*

Build one district of about 250 m square: a main street with a bend, two side streets and a back lane, giving one crossroads, two T-junctions and four blocks; a small square; and a main street that climbs a few metres so runs must step. Put 25 to 30 buildings on it in three ways. Terraced rows along the main street come from a facade kit through recipes (wall, door bay, window bay, end, convex and concave corner, filler, ground and top ledge, two storeys). Four or five detached buildings are single hand-made models with declared footprint, front and door connector, the hand-authored escape hatch FINDINGS asks for. One is an arcade or gatehouse with a passable footprint, and one is enterable, joining an interior through a door connector. Street furniture comes only from street recipes (lamps at an interval, bins at junctions).

Build it twice from one source. First with a proof kit of about a dozen untextured boxes at exact module and pivot, which is Bethesda's proof stage and costs a day; then with a real kit swapped in through the pack. The module (for instance a 4 m bay and 3 m storey) is the user's choice, not a standard.

It exercises the frame and front rule, module against bounds, the pivot check, roles and variants, corners and ends, the remainder policy, storey stacking, connectors, passable footprints, street-owned dressing, derived blocks, frontage, and the greybox-to-kit swap. It passes when the following hold, each reported with the protocol that measured it:

| Test | Passing result |
|---|---|
| Clean compile | Zero errors with both packs, from the same world source |
| Seams | Measured gap or overlap between neighbouring bays within the declared tolerance, computed from geometry, not judged from a render |
| Kit self-tests | Loopback closes around a rectangular and an L-shaped footprint; two storeys stack with no coincident faces |
| Structure | Every building has a frontage and a door reachable on foot from the square; every block is derived, none drawn |
| Output | Every placed thing is an explicit instance with a stable id; a second compile is identical |
| Revised piece, geometry only | A wall piece is remodelled within the same contract: no instance moves, only the lock records a new measurement |
| Revised piece, contract | The wall's module changes from 4 m to 4.5 m, or its pivot moves from centre to corner: the compiler names every affected run and instance, says which runs no longer close and what lengths would, and nothing shifts silently |
| Moved street | The main street is shifted 3 m sideways: its lamps and bins follow; buildings fronting it either follow or are reported as off their frontage; blocks re-derive; keyed overrides in the untouched blocks survive and any in the touched block are kept or flagged; the instance diff is confined to the blocks that street bounds |

The last three rows are the point of the exercise. Sections 1 and 3 show that building the town is the solved part and that surviving a change is what studios pay for.

## 7. What is uncertain, and which conclusions rest on it

The notes were single bounded passes. Talk videos and most slide decks were not watched or read; several pages were truncated; and source wording reached the notes through a summarising fetch tool.

**Seen only in search summaries.** The Unreal module sizes (100 cm grid, 400 by 300 cm walls) and the claim that corner pivots are near-universal come from a forum post and a vendor blog; the report therefore treats module size and pivot location as user choices and the dogfood's 4 m by 3 m as an example. Buildify's role-by-collection scheme and wall stretching, the Houdini `Base*Variant` naming, Unity's `_LOD` suffix, the Vanegas parcel-subdivision methods, the origin paper for CGA (Müller et al. 2006), and all details for Cesium, Esri and Cities: Skylines importers are summary-only. Whether Unreal's grammar nodes first shipped in 5.4 or 5.5 is unresolved.

**Third-party notes on talks.** Every Far Cry 5 detail, the Spider-Man description of offline generation with hand polish, and the Assassin's Creed Unity block generator (a fan reading of video frames). The games table's rows for those three should be read accordingly. Spider-Man's retention of hand work that still meets its specification is from the SIGGRAPH abstract, which does not explain the mechanism; change 10 borrows the idea, not a documented design.

**Recalled, not read.** All IFC claims; CityGML 3.0's level-of-detail definitions, building attributes and transportation classes; OpenDRIVE lane detail; several OSM tags. The vocabulary table in section 2 relies on pages that were read (OSM, CityJSON, Parish and Müller, the OpenDRIVE overview) except where marked, but the statement that storey is IFC's primary container is recalled.

**Negative findings from limited searches.** "No standard for kit metadata", "no typed connector vocabulary", "no documented migration mechanism for a revised kit piece" and "no tool merges edits of generated output" are absences from one pass each. Not checked: USD schema work at the Alliance for OpenUSD, Overture Maps (which likely defines segments and connectors), cadastral standards that define parcels, and Esri rule packages. Hand-edit persistence in CityEngine, PCG and Houdini beyond per-floor overrides was not verified, so the claim that edits survive only upstream rests on Townscaper, Dungeon Architect, the Houdini per-floor overrides and the Wildlands interview. If CityEngine's local edits work as recalled, they are a further instance of keyed overrides and strengthen change 10 without changing it.

**Gaps that bear on the recommendations.** No exterior facade kit's piece list was found; the role list in sections 5 and 6 is assembled from tool slots (Houdini, Buildify) and interior kit practice, and a real facade kit may need roles not listed. No studio source shows a lot layer, which is why lot is deferred, but Horizon, Kingdom Come, later Assassin's Creed titles and Watch Dogs Legion were not researched. Unity's world axes were not confirmed from a primary page. On the framework's side, FINDINGS records that the three `module_m` values were set by judgement and that bay joins were never measured, so the claim that the current kit run produces clean seams is itself untested.

## Conclusion

The research reverses an easy assumption. The hard part of a modular city is not a richer asset format or a cleverer generator: assets stay nearly mute in every tool examined, and no shipped settlement was found that a generator finished. The hard part is the unwritten contract between a piece and the thousands of places it is used, and what happens to those places when the contract or the street beneath them moves. A text-first compiler is well placed for exactly that, because its facts are declared, its measurements are repeatable and its output is a diffable list of instances.

The practical consequence is an ordering. Write the frame and the kit contract down first, then make revisions accountable through a lock and a diff, and only then add recipes and street structure. A framework that can say precisely which instances a moved pivot affects, and which hand overrides survived a moved street, offers something no source in this research describes a studio having.
